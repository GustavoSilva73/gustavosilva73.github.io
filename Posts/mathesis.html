The Specter of Mathesis: Structure, Substance, and the Quarrel over the Foundations of Mathematics

Published: 2026-11-25 · Full treatise: PDF, 115 pp.

“Numbers […] are known only by their laws, the laws of arithmetic, so that any constructs obeying those laws—certain sets, for instance—are eligible […] explications of number. […] there is no saying absolutely what the numbers are; there is only arithmetic.” — W. V. O. Quine, Ontological Relativity (1969)

0. Two Problems under One Name

Mathesis universalis has always been a name for two problems that ought to remain distinct and are habitually conflated. The first concerns the feasibility of systematising mathematical knowledge within a comprehensive formal structure: whether the heterogeneous branches of mathematics can be built and articulated from a common ground. The second concerns the epistemic authority conferred upon mathematical formalisation when it presumes to dictate what counts as rigorous or scientifically valid knowledge. The first is my primary concern; the second will be addressed only once the first has been given a precise form. Between them runs the question that accompanies this whole essay: whether the unity of mathematics is to be attained through a common conception of its subjects, or through the relations, transformations and invariants that define mathematical constructs. The contemporary quarrel over foundations—the set-theoretic paradigm, identified here with ZFC, on one side, and the categorical and homotopical methodologies cultivated over recent decades on the other—is, I contend, the quintessential arena in which this question is now being decided. My hypothesis is that category theory and, more strongly, Homotopy Type Theory (HoTT) are the contemporary frameworks most propitious for reconsidering the modernist ambition of a mathesis. Three disclaimers bound the hypothesis from the outset. It presumes no direct historical continuity between these frameworks and the projects of early modernity, nor does it regard them as the realisation of those projects; it offers no definitive resolution of the question of mathematical unity; and the ontological readings adopted here are peculiar to this essay, since ZFC does not of itself entail a substantialist account of mathematical objects—it may equally underpin structuralist readings—and categories or types do not, solely by dint of their formal architecture, establish an entirely relational ontology.

The story usually begins with Descartes. In the Regulae ad directionem ingenii he seeks a general method for the discovery of truth, impelled by dissatisfaction with the heterogeneity of the mathematics of his day, and he names mathesis universalis the general science through which any object may be considered, provided it admits of a determinate order and measure. The development of the calculus then sharpened the difficulty: in Leibniz’s notation the differentials stand in a formal relation whose significance cannot be read off by treating each symbol as an isolated entity, and the long debate over infinitesimals afforded Leibniz the generative matrix for his metamathematical projects—a characteristica universalis, a calculus ratiocinator and an ars combinatoria. Following Foucault’s examination of the classical épistémè in Les mots et les choses, I keep these projects apart from the different enterprise of rendering nature calculable through mathematical and mechanical models; Foucault serves here to clarify the conceptual ground on which the later foundational debates unfold, not as a normative model for present-day mathematics. With this distinction in place, the argument can be stated as a single path. It begins (§I) by recovering from the history of mathesis a standard of unity precise enough to be tested; it then tests that standard successively against the cumulative hierarchy (§II), against categories and toposes (§III) and against homotopy types (§IV); and it ends (§V) by formalising what survives the test as a reinterpreted mathesis universalis.

I. The Problem before the Foundations: From the Katholou to the Focus Imaginarius

To know what a foundation must deliver, one must first know what ‘unity’ has been taken to mean, and here I rely largely on Rabouin, who argues that no rupture can be claimed for Descartes. The concept predates classical modernity and has its genesis among the Ancients, in philosophical reflection on the unity of mathematics (as opposed to the mathematisation of nature); and, ironically, its decisive formulation comes from Aristotle, whom Rabouin calls the enemy, if ever there was one, of the promoters of the modern mathesis. The question arises because, for Plato, the dialectician is distinguished by a synoptic vision that gathers the branches of knowledge into a unified view, and this requires that the mathêmata display a certain unity by themselves, lest the unity appear as an interpretation already postulated by the philosopher. Aristotle’s objection is that pure mathematics presents no such transparent unity, since it contains theories irreducible to one another: a demonstration valid for numbers cannot be transposed to magnitudes, which is precisely the situation in Euclid’s Elements. Arithmetic and geometry treat of ontologically distinct genera, and any universal science—logic, dialectic, metaphysics—can operate only externally to those genera. Rabouin places at the heart of his study the question this leaves behind: what status can the universal have in mathematics, and is a ‘universal’ standpoint possible at all? [PDF, Ch. 3 §I.1]

Aristotle’s answer is not a simple refusal, and its texture matters for everything that follows. He concedes that mathematics displays a certain community, since all its disciplines fall under a single category, quantity, and since Book X of the Elements treats numbers and magnitudes jointly through proportion. Unity may therefore be sought either de l’extérieur, through a theory that is logical or philosophical rather than mathematical, or de l’intérieur, through a theory arising from within mathematics; but the second horn would require, by the criteria of the Posterior Analytics, a domain of objects and principles at once ‘proper’ and ‘common’, which his epistemology excludes. His strategy is to turn the very notion of a universal mathematics against the idea of a separate universal: a universal mathematics bearing on a general object would require a third type of substance, neither Idea nor mathematical object, neither number, point, magnitude nor time (Metaph. M 2), whereas the universal propositions of mathematics bear on magnitudes and numbers themselves, considered not as possessing magnitude or as divisible (Metaph. M 3). Rabouin warns against reading this through the modern notion of abstraction, as though it yielded a universal object called ‘quantity’: the universal need not be separated, nor constitute a ‘common subject’.

The example of measure shows why the community cannot harden into a science. For Aristotle the One arises as the measure of quantity, and the arithmetical unit, the monas, is its most exact model, since it is posited as an absolute indivisible; yet measure is always of the same genus as what it measures—magnitude for magnitudes, length for lengths (Metaph. I 1). A geometrical unit can be fragmented: one speaks of half a metre, or of half a foot, the foot being indivisible only for perception, since every continuum is divisible. The number 1 is not so divisible. The geometrical unit and the arithmetical unit are therefore heterogeneous, each homogeneous only within its own genus: an analogy between them sacrifices the indivisibility of the One, whereas the point cannot measure magnitudes without violating homogeneity. What is gained on one side is lost on the other, and a ‘mathematical unit’ becomes a philosophical analogy of metatheoretical character rather than an effective unification internal to mathematics. The same structure governs relations. The earlier doctrines—the Pythagorean privilege of numerical ratios, the Platonic Dyad of the Great and the Small, one of whose names was the Unequal—converge on a theory of the Equal and the Unequal, with relations as the basic bricks of the universe. Aristotle opposes it on the ground that a relation cannot be conceived without something else that serves as its subject (Metaph. N 1). General rules of equality and proportion do obtain in mathematics, but no ‘superscience’ of abstract relations may be created, since operations have no meaning outside the domains of objects on which they act. Without a concrete genus beneath it, a relation has no subject to rest upon. This tension between objects and relations is the one that returns, transformed, in §§II–IV.

The target of the criticism makes the point vivid. Speusippus, having retained only mathematical things among his principles, sought unity through the analogy by which the point stands to the line as the one to number (Top. I 18). Aristotle accepts the analogical reasoning, even as a standard procedure for constituting a common genus, but refuses its ontological conclusion: the independence of arithmetic and geometry precludes any such genus, and the Speusippean world becomes, in Rabouin’s gloss, a bad tragedy whose episodes do not follow from one another (Metaph. N 3). Yet Aristotle defends another model, the universal demonstration of An. Post. I 5, whose examples are exclusively mathematical: the permutability of proportions had once been demonstrated separately for numbers, lines, solids and times, for lack of a single name for what they share, whereas ‘now’ the proof is universal, holding of them no longer as lines or as numbers. Rabouin cautions that the passage settles neither the mathematical or purely logical character of that demonstration nor whether its ‘now’ is temporal or logical, and he shows that the example cuts both ways: an interlocutor may argue that a demonstration bearing on what does not exist apart from particulars is inferior to the particular one (An. Post. I 24), to which Aristotle replies that nothing compels one to suppose the universal a separate reality. In An. Post. II 17 the same example returns, the cause of permutability differing for lines and for numbers and yet being the same ‘as implying a determinate increase’—a phrase Rabouin takes as the first positive, if nominal, designation of the level of the katholou. He refuses to dissolve the tension, preferring to let the problem of the universal in mathematics live rather than crush it beneath an ad hoc solution. For Aristotle, then, universality in mathematics shows itself in universal demonstrations and in a qualified sameness of cause across genera, yet never coalesces into an autonomous science, since ontology forbids detaching relational form from its generic substrate. This is the first notion of mathesis: an intratheoretical universality, to be kept distinct from what follows.

Descartes inherits the question rather than inventing it: the theme of a general mathematics had been revitalised after the rediscovery of Proclus’s commentary on Euclid, Van Roomen (1597) and Alsted (1613) had used the expression before him, and Rule IV of the Regulae presents it under a name already ancient and in received use. His distinctive move, on Rabouin’s reading, is to articulate the order studied by the mathesis, which classifies relations as ordo and mensura, with the order of method (Rule VI), without identifying the two. Rule IV assigns to the mathesis whatever admits of an examination of order or measure—numbers, figures, stars, sounds—as a general science of order and measure nulli speciali materiae addictas. The generality is narrower than it sounds: the list of objects remains thoroughly mathematical, what is sought is a measure rather than an order, and freedom from any particular matter is not freedom from all matter, since every question is referred to a ‘common subject’, extension abstracted from everything save its being figured. What Aristotle had dismissed as impossible—a science of pure relations without a determinate genus—is therefore not yet realised; the Aristotelian demand for a subject is displaced onto a homogeneous extension that the imagination can display. Nor should this displacement be located, as many historians have done, on the first page of La Géométrie (1637), where algebraic notation seems to fuse arithmetic and geometry. That identification is owed to a historical accident—van Schooten’s course of 1651, transcribed by Erasmus Bartholin as the Principia Matheseos Universalis, led a great part of the seventeenth century, Leibniz included, to treat the mathesis and algebra applied to geometry as at least isomorphic. Descartes himself refused the amalgam, writing to Ciermans that the treatise explained none of the questions properly belonging to arithmetic, nor those in which order and measure are considered; and the Regulae neither contain the treatment of curves by equations nor secure the unity of the discrete and the continuous. [PDF, Ch. 3 §I.2]

This gap between the texts weakens the image, forged at the end of the nineteenth century, of a unified classical mathesis governing the whole Classical Age, an image Foucault still echoes when he places such a mathesis, with algebra as its universal method, at one pole of the classical épistémè. But Foucault’s object is the epistemological organisation of classical knowledge, not Descartes’s personal mathematics, and it is useful precisely as such. Descartes, on this reading, rejects resemblance not by excluding it from thought but by subjecting it to the test of comparison, and there are only two forms of comparison: measure and order, the former reducible to the latter. Measure compares through an external common unit: two magnitudes \(A\) and \(C\) that cannot be compared directly become comparable once a unit \(u\) is found with \(A=au\), \(C=cu\), so that the comparison reduces to a relation between \(a\) and \(c\) (Rule XII). With due poetic licence, the essay illustrates this by the Morita equivalence of Poisson manifolds. Three Poisson manifolds \((M_A,\pi_A)\), \((M_B,\pi_B)\), \((M_C,\pi_C)\) need be neither diffeomorphic nor of the same dimension, so no direct Poisson diffeomorphism is available; following Xu, \(M_A\) and \(M_B\) are compared through a third term, a symplectic bimodule \((S_{AB},\omega_{AB})\) forming a dual pair

where \(\alpha_1\) is a complete Poisson map and \(\beta_1\) a complete anti-Poisson map, both surjective submersions with connected and simply connected, symplectically orthogonal fibres. If \(M_B\) and \(M_C\) are likewise linked, the bridges compose: since \(\beta_1\) and \(\alpha_2\) are submersions, the fibre product \(S_{AB}\times_{M_B}S_{BC}\) is a coisotropic submanifold of \((S_{AB}\times S_{BC},\,\omega_{AB}\oplus\omega_{BC})\), and its quotient by the characteristic foliation, \(S_{AC}=(S_{AB}\times_{M_B}S_{BC})/\mathcal{F}\), is a symplectic manifold defining a dual pair between \(M_A\) and \(M_C\). The common unit is the symplectic manifold that joins them, and in the composite case it arises from joining two bimodules. Order, by contrast, needs no exteriority: the compared terms alone suffice, one identifying the simplest and establishing order through the relations of dependence or succession that bind the others to it, the ‘absolute’ and the ‘relative’ being defined by reference to the aim of knowledge (Rule VI). Measure reduces to order because arithmetic is itself a series from the simplest, the unit, to the more complex; in a chain \(A\to B\to C\to D\to\cdots\), knowing \(A\) one may know \(B\), and from \(B\), \(C\), so that knowledge becomes an ordered chain of inferences disposed not by genera of being but insofar as some things can be known from others. Here difference, rather than resemblance, becomes the principle of intelligibility. Rabouin adds two cautions: the extension of this rationality to non-quantitative objects belongs to the method, not to the mathesis, which Descartes introduces through thoroughly mathematical objects; and the chain is the point of departure of the method, not its outcome.

On Foucault’s arrangement, what makes a body of classical knowledge possible is its relation to a knowledge of order. Simple natures fall to the mathesis, whose universal method is algebra; complex representations, which require a system of signs, fall to taxinomia; signs stand to composite natures as algebra stands to simple ones, and the science of order divides accordingly:

\[ \textit{Science générale de l’ordre}\;:\qquad \underbrace{\text{natures simples}}_{\textit{Mathesis}\ \to\ \text{Algèbre}}\qquad\Big|\qquad \underbrace{\text{représentations complexes}}_{\textit{Taxinomia}\ \to\ \text{Signes}} \]

Since the simple is only a particular case of the complex—the absolute being such only relative to an aim (Rule VI)—one may equally say that mathesis is a particular case of taxinomia, setting aside the few pure natures that remain the same in any series. And because what we know is a relatively simple element of a more complex structure still to be known, taxinomy presupposes a hidden continuity of things and a power of imagination by which it can be reconstructed from discontinuous representations. Enter genèse, the analysis of the constitution of order from empirical series, which Foucault places at the opposite extreme of the épistémè, with the region of signs extending between it and the mathesis—as if, in the language of Morita equivalence, the signs were the symplectic bimodule and mathesis and genèse the Poisson manifolds, the signs organising representations into a simultaneous table of identities and differences without leaving the domain of representation. This reconstruction must not be taken as Descartes’s own conception. For Descartes rejects purely formal reasoning: order and measure remain anchored in a transparency secured by exhibiting relations and proportions on the support of spatial imagination, in what Rabouin calls a logic of imagination.

Leibniz does not break with the imagination either, since mathematical objects remain for him clear and distinct ideas subject to it; the divergence from Descartes lies instead in the status of the sign. Thought, for Leibniz, is an economical suspension of explanation: we do not intuit complex things in all their parts at once, but rerum loco signis utimur. Thinking of a chiliagon, one does not continuously consider what ‘side’, ‘equality’ and ‘thousand’ mean, but uses the words loco idearum, remembering only that their signification is at hand. Symbolic knowledge, under which algebra and arithmetic fall, is thus cogitatio caeca, blind thought, and the sign acquires genuine operative autonomy, allowing demonstrations to conclude vi formae even under partial conceptual resolution. Its semantic autonomy, however, is limited. The meaning of a character is not generated by syntactic manipulation but held in provisional deferral, within the subject’s power (in potestate) to be exhibited by analysis, and is validated outside the calculus—a posteriori through experience, or a priori through causal definition and resolution into compatible requisita. The essay’s analogy is the mathematised physical theory: its mathematics may hold together internally, yet it makes sense only if it corresponds to objects external to its formalism. Leibniz built no single project under the name mathesis universalis, but three: an ars combinatoria decomposing complex concepts into simpler ones and recombining them; a characteristica universalis, an alphabetum cogitationum humanarum representing simple and complex concepts in one symbolic language; and a calculus ratiocinator, the rules by which characters are manipulated and new conclusions drawn from old—none of them reducible to formal logic or to mathematics. The project carried three difficulties: it presupposes that primitive concepts can be identified, which Leibniz concedes may be impossible; correct operation within the calculus does not guarantee that what the characters represent is possible or true; and, most deeply, if every truth is true because the predicate is contained in the concept of the subject, it is unclear how any truth can be contingent. Leibniz answers by distinguishing finite analysis, which reduces necessary truths to identity \(A=A\) under the principle of non-contradiction, from infinite analysis, in which the complete concept of a contingent truth converges only asymptotically, its completed limit apprehensible solely by an infinite intellect, under the principle of sufficient reason. [PDF, Ch. 3 §I.2]

Kant breaks the classical mathesis at exactly this hinge. He accepts the implication Leibniz resisted—if the predicate is contained in the subject, the judgement is necessary and its negation contradictory—but restricts it to analytic judgements, so that the finite intellect’s inability to complete the series of substitutions marks only its ignorance before a hidden necessity; and where the predicate cannot be extracted by dissecting the concept, subject and predicate are joined not by identity but by synthesis, through a third term external to the concept, pure or empirical intuition. He then strikes at the discipline that inspired Leibniz. Leibniz had begun from the scale of identity—\(67+67=134\), demonstrable by substitution of definitions—and extended it to the infinite identity of contingent reason; Kant holds that mathematical propositions are synthetic a priori, mathematical cognition proceeding by the construction of concepts in the pure intuition of space and time. With arithmetic and geometry removed from the regime of formal identity, infinite analysis is displaced to the regulative plane: the series of empirical conditions is never given as a completed totality, but set as a task in a regress in indefinitum, and the complete convergence of empirical laws into a single system becomes a focus imaginarius, a regulative Idea guiding inquiry without ever becoming a determinate object. The Critique of Judgement completes the move, leaving the manifold of particular laws contingent and making their lawfulness thinkable through the reflecting power of judgement and its subjective principle of purposiveness. The classical mathesis thereby forfeits its premise of a single science of identity.

From this history I assemble the conception of mathesis that the rest of the essay tests. It retains the Ancient understanding of mathematics as an internally unified domain; it extends the demand for unity to scientific knowledge generally, adopting systematic coherence as a regulative standard, in debt to Leibniz’s aspiration but without his assumption of a completely given totality, since it adopts Kant’s restriction that systematic unity is an ideal guiding inquiry, not a constitutive property of its object. Mathematics thus provides at once an internally unified domain and an exemplary standard of rigour—conceptual determination, explicit principles, demonstrative relations, systematic coherence—without any reduction of every science to mathematical formalisation. One further constraint is needed, because the Ancient conception is too diffuse, and in a sense too ahistorical, to be confronted with contemporary foundations. It comes from Cavaillès, for whom mathematics develops through an immanent dynamic in which concepts, operations and structures generate new problems and forms of intelligibility: not along a straight line towards a predetermined horizon, but from a necessity internal to the discipline, through internal tensions—something close to Adorno’s idea of the formal unity of autonomous works of art. This is the criterion that will decide between the foundations. They are not to be treated as competing systems, nor as stages in an inevitable progression, but examined for how far each articulates these internal tensions; and the question to be put to each is whether the unity it offers is merely extensional, or genetic—a unity in which one stage engenders the next through the thematisation of its own operations. [PDF, Ch. 3 §I.2]

II. Substance: The Cumulative Hierarchy and Its Limits

The first foundation to face this standard is also the one under which most contemporary mathematics is actually formalised, and its origins already contain the problem. Cantor’s set theory grew out of his work on Fourier series \(\sum a_i\sin nx\): the operation of forming the set of limit points could be iterated transfinitely, \(P_0,P_1,\dots,P_\omega,\dots,P_{\omega+\omega},\dots\), giving rise to actual infinity and transfinite numbers. The attempt to found this theory rigorously belonged to the logicist movement, and above all to Frege, whose Begriffsschrift (1879) replaced subject–predicate analysis with the distinction of function and argument, and whose Grundlagen (1884) characterised number without taking it as primitive: two concepts have the same number when their extensions can be put in bijection, \(\#F=\#G\iff F\approx G\)—a logicist anticipation, in naive substantialist form, of the structural equivalence that univalence will later make internal. In the Grundgesetze (1893) Basic Law V, \(\mathrm{Ext}(F)=\mathrm{Ext}(G)\iff\forall x\,(F(x)\leftrightarrow G(x))\), allowed every expressible property to determine an extension, and the concept \(R(x)\iff x\notin x\) with \(r=\mathrm{Ext}(R)\) yields \(r\in r\iff R(r)\iff r\notin r\). Russell communicated the contradiction to Frege on 16 June 1902, as the second volume was being prepared; Frege’s appendix failed to save the system, and an axiomatisation became necessary. Zermelo’s of 1908 severed mathematics from unrestricted comprehension by the simple observation that a property should select only from the elements of a set already given. Two later remedies are worth noting in advance, since they foreshadow the argument: categorical logic resolves the problem by internalising properties as monomorphisms classified by a subobject classifier \(\Omega\), shifting from a material to a structural determination of subsets, while in HoTT membership is tied to types and the paradoxical self-reference cannot even be articulated. [PDF, Ch. 1 §I.1]

The notion of set that results is iterative, and this is what makes it object-based. In Boolos’s illustration, stage 0 contains only the empty set; at each successor stage every possible collection of the objects formed so far is formed; at each limit stage everything is gathered. Formally, by transfinite recursion, \[ V_0=\varnothing,\qquad V_{\alpha+1}=\mathcal{P}(V_\alpha),\qquad V_\lambda=\bigcup_{\alpha<\lambda}V_\alpha\ \ (\lambda\text{ limit}),\qquad V=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\mathrm{Ord}}V_\alpha, \] so that \(V_1=\{\varnothing\}\), \(V_2=\{\varnothing,\{\varnothing\}\}\), \(V_3=\{\varnothing,\{\varnothing\},\{\{\varnothing\}\},\{\varnothing,\{\varnothing\}\}\}\), and, since \(|V_{n+1}|=2^{|V_n|}\), the sizes run \(0,1,2,4,16,65536\). The construction is the exact analogue of von Neumann’s naturals, \(0=\varnothing\), \(n+1=n\cup\{n\}\), with the power set in place of the successor; the first limit stage \(V_\omega\) contains all hereditarily finite sets, each natural \(n\) sitting at \(n\in V_{n+1}\setminus V_n\). Every level is transitive and the levels are cumulative, \(V_\beta\subseteq V_\alpha\) for \(\beta\le\alpha\). The converse question—whether every set appears at some level—is answered by the rank, \(\mathrm{rank}(x)=\sup\{\mathrm{rank}(y)+1\mid y\in x\}\), with \(x\in V_\alpha\iff\mathrm{rank}(x)<\alpha\); thus \(\mathrm{rank}(\{\{\varnothing\}\})=2\), the same as \(\mathrm{rank}(2)\), two distinct sets sharing a level, and \(\mathrm{rank}(\omega)=\omega\), so \(\omega\in V_{\omega+1}\setminus V_\omega\). That every set has a rank is no free gift: working in ZF without Foundation, Kunen establishes \(\text{Foundation}\iff\forall A\,(\in\text{ is well-founded on }A)\iff V=\mathrm{WF}\). Foundation is also what forbids \(x\in x\), which would give \(\mathrm{rank}(x)\ge\mathrm{rank}(x)+1\); and it is not forced upon us, since Aczel’s Anti-Foundation Axiom admits \(x=\{x\}\) and settles the identity of a set by its pattern of membership rather than by its position in a stratified construction. In the standard picture, however, the identity of a set is decided by its place in the hierarchy, and the objects that constitute sets are ontologically prior to the sets themselves. [PDF, Ch. 1 §II]

How much of ZFC does this picture justify? It delivers Extensionality (each level is transitive, so a set is determined by sets formed before it), Separation (any subcollection of a level is formed at the next), Pairing and Union (if \(x,y\in V_\alpha\) then \(\{x,y\}\in V_{\alpha+1}\), and \(\bigcup x\in V_{\alpha+1}\) whenever \(x\in V_\alpha\)), Power Set, which is the very successor step, and Foundation. It does not deliver Infinity, since \(V_\omega\) satisfies every other axiom: the picture says how to climb, not that a limit level exists to be reached. Nor does it deliver Replacement: \(V_{\omega+\omega}\) is a model of Zermelo’s theory, yet \(n\mapsto V_{\omega+n}\) has a range cofinal in \(V_{\omega+\omega}\) and hence not a member of it—exactly the gap between Zermelo’s system and the one completed by Fraenkel and Skolem. Those two axioms must rest on other grounds, and the essay is frank about what they are. Self-evidence will not do: Maddy recalls that the excluded middle was once ‘self-evident’, and that the principle that every property determines a set proved false. What remains is fecundity—ZFC answered Russell’s paradox and has carried a century of mathematics—together with comfort and inertia: ‘everything functions as it should; why alter it now?’ This is not without merit, but practical stability is not absolute assurance, and ZFC’s endurance is better understood as the outcome of historical achievement, formal adaptability and institutional familiarity than as testimony to a privileged epistemological standing. [PDF, Ch. 1 §I.2]

The shape of the hierarchy shows the limits of this object-based ontology more precisely. Its height is handled elegantly: every set lies at some level, the levels are indexed by all of \(\mathrm{Ord}\), there is no last one, and under Foundation Russell’s class \(\{x\mid x\notin x\}\) coincides with \(V\), so that the paradox reduces to the fact that \(V\) is a proper class. Its width, however, is fixed only by the Power Set axiom, independently of height. \(|V_\omega|=\aleph_0\) and \(|V_{\omega+1}|=2^{\aleph_0}\), the cardinality of the reals; in general \(|V_{\omega+\alpha}|=\beth_\alpha\). The Continuum Hypothesis \(2^{\aleph_0}=\aleph_1\) therefore asks precisely how wide the hierarchy is at stage \(\omega+1\)—whether \(\mathcal{P}(V_\omega)\) is as small as it could be—and ZFC does not decide it: assuming consistency, Gödel showed through the constructible universe that it does not refute CH, and Cohen through forcing that it does not prove it. ZFC settles how deep the hierarchy goes but not how wide it is at the first stage beyond \(V_\omega\); the aspiration to a complete, rigid and finished ontology of objects fails both in depth, since no level is last, and in width, since the axioms do not fix the size of a level. One precision keeps the thesis honest. In ZFC the ‘objects’ antecedent to sets are themselves sets, ultimately the empty set; object-priority acquires its literal form only in ZFU, where \(V_0(A)=A\) is a stock of atoms with no elements, \(V_{\alpha+1}(A)=V_\alpha(A)\cup\mathcal{P}(V_\alpha(A))\), and the first sets are collections of objects given beforehand. Taking \(A=\varnothing\) recovers the pure hierarchy, and the pure sets of any model of ZFU form a model of ZFC. ZFU thus locates the object-based reading rather than refuting it, exhibiting as its very epitome the ontological model that ZFC carries in pure form. [PDF, Ch. 1 §II]

We can now put the question of §I to set theory. Its answer to the demand for unity is direct: every mathematical object is, or is encoded by, a set—ordered pairs as \((a,b):=\{\{a\},\{a,b\}\}\), functions as sets of pairs, \(\mathbb{N}\) as \(\omega\), \(\mathbb{R}\) by Dedekind cuts or classes of Cauchy sequences, a group as a tuple \((G,m,e,\iota)\in V\) satisfying the axioms. The resulting unification is reductive in two senses, ontological (one kind of entity, one primitive relation \(\in\)) and representational (every object admits an encoding in a common medium), though what is reduced is the kinds of entity, not the plurality of structures, which persist as sets equipped with further sets. And the unity does not reach the identity of objects. This is Benacerraf’s point in What Numbers Could Not Be, told as a parable. Ernie learns that \(0=\varnothing\) and that the successor of \(n\) is \(n\cup\{n\}\), so that \(1=\{\varnothing\}\), \(2=\{\varnothing,\{\varnothing\}\}\); Johnny learns that the successor of \(n\) is \(\{n\}\), so that \(2=\{\{\varnothing\}\}\), \(3=\{\{\{\varnothing\}\}\}\). Both count, add and multiply, and agree on every arithmetical truth; they disagree only on questions arithmetic does not concern itself with, such as whether \(3\in 17\)—true for Ernie, since 3 is an element of every larger number, false for Johnny, since the only element of 17 is 16. Neither can give an arithmetical reason to prefer his choice, for every such reason applies equally to the other, and Benacerraf concludes that no particular set can be the number 3. In the essay’s notation, with \(S_N(x)=x\cup\{x\}\) and \(S_Z(x)=\{x\}\), both progressions satisfy Peano, yet \(1_N\in 3_N\) while \(1_Z\notin 3_Z\): a set-theoretic statement about ‘the number 3’ can be true under one encoding and false under another, so the set-theoretic constitution of an object cannot fix its mathematical identity. [PDF, Ch. 3 §II.1]

Yet the conception of unity adopted here does not require that unity be a matter of the identity of objects, and the decisive question lies elsewhere: does the construction of all objects from a common basis exhaust the unity of mathematics as a developing rational domain, in Cavaillès’s sense? My answer is negative—not because set theory cannot formalise relations, operations or structures, which it does with extraordinary power and fertility, but because the cumulative hierarchy and the axioms generating it supply a common substrate for representing and constructing entities, not an account of the immanent dynamics by which concepts and structures develop. That a structure is representable in \(V\) does not explain the internal tensions from which new concepts emerge, and I take this to be connected, at least in part, with the object-based orientation: even perfectly represented relations remain grounded in an ontology whose fundamental units are sets. Set theory is not devoid of internal development—large cardinals, inner models and forcing generate hierarchies of consistency strength and families of models—but these act on \(V\) and its extensions from the outside, driven by independence phenomena and by the adoption of new axioms, and do not follow from the cumulative principle itself. ZFC gives mathematics a common basis for representation and construction; it does not give an account of how its web of relations transforms itself. Benacerraf has meanwhile pointed in the direction the next step must take: what arithmetic describes is a common structure, in which a number is a place that something occupies, not an object whose nature can be inspected apart from that place.

III. Arrows: Individuation up to Isomorphism

Benacerraf’s distinction between the elements of a particular realisation and the positions they occupy has a familiar counterpart in physics. The Einstein–Maxwell equations \(\mathrm{Ric}-\mathrm{scal}\,g=\kappa T^{\mathrm{EM}}\) are not themselves spacetime but a mathematical object used to describe it; and the Reissner–Nordström metric, a static, spherically symmetric spacetime of mass \(M\) and charge \(Q\), modelling a black hole when \(M^2\ge Q^2\), may be written either as \[ g=-f(r)\,dt\otimes dt+f^{-1}(r)\,dr\otimes dr+r^2(d\theta\otimes d\theta+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi\otimes d\phi),\qquad f(r)=1-\tfrac{2M}{r}+\tfrac{Q^2}{r^2}, \] or, equivalently, as \(g=-\vartheta^0\otimes\vartheta^0+\vartheta^1\otimes\vartheta^1+\vartheta^2\otimes\vartheta^2+\vartheta^3\otimes\vartheta^3\) in the orthogonal coframe \(\vartheta^0=\sqrt{f}\,dt\), \(\vartheta^1=f^{-1/2}\,dr\), \(\vartheta^2=r\,d\theta\), \(\vartheta^3=r\sin\theta\,d\phi\). Neither expression is a black hole; neither is a privileged representation of it, and there is no ‘representational hierarchy’ between them. In the same way \(\{\varnothing,\{\varnothing\}\}\) is no privileged representation of 2, but one realisation of the arithmetic structure among others, such as Zermelo’s. Awodey extends the point: the working mathematician studies structures and the relations between positions within them, whereas philosophers of ‘structure’ have largely ignored how such objects are actually constructed in the discipline from which they draw inspiration. [PDF, Ch. 2 §III]

Their structures are typically Bourbaki models \(\mathfrak{B}=\langle A;\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F},\mathcal{C};\Phi\rangle\), with an underlying set, families of relations, operations and distinguished constants, and an axiom schema. The trouble is that this notion is syntax-dependent: it relies on a prior choice, external to the structure, of the relations, operations and constants through which it is formalised. Topological spaces are the paradigm case. A topology on a set \(X\) can be presented by its open sets \(\mathcal{O}(X)\), closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections; by its closed sets \(\mathcal{C}(X)\), closed under arbitrary intersections and finite unions; or by a closure operator with \(\mathrm{cl}(\varnothing)=\varnothing\), \(S\subseteq\mathrm{cl}(S)\), \(\mathrm{cl}(S\cup T)=\mathrm{cl}(S)\cup\mathrm{cl}(T)\) and \(\mathrm{cl}\circ\mathrm{cl}=\mathrm{cl}\)—three distinct Bourbaki structures for one mathematical structure. Category theory supersedes this dependence. It emerged elsewhere, in Eilenberg and Mac Lane’s work on natural transformations and algebraic topology, not as a rival foundation but as a general language for structures and the mappings between them; and in that language the definition of a category itself determines a structure, its objects being positions defined entirely through the class of admissible, structure-preserving morphisms, with isomorphism as the criterion of structural identity. A category can even be axiomatised in terms of arrows alone, objects being recovered as units of composition.

The mechanism that replaces encoding by role is the universal property. A universal arrow from \(c\) to a functor \(G:\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{C}\) is a pair \(\langle r,u:c\to Gr\rangle\) through which every \(f:c\to Gd\) factors uniquely as \(Gf'\circ u\). The free vector space is one: for any set \(X\), the space \(V_X\) of formal \(K\)-linear combinations of elements of \(X\) comes with \(j:X\to U(V_X)\) such that any \(f:X\to U(W)\) extends to a unique linear map \(f':V_X\to W\) with \(Uf'\circ j=f\). The completion of a metric space is another: for every \(X\) in the category of metric spaces and non-expansive maps there is an isometric embedding \(\eta_X:X\to U(\overline{X})\) with dense image into a complete space, through which every non-expansive map into a complete space factors uniquely. Such objects are characterised entirely by what they do to maps, never by what they are made of. Limits are universal in the same way (a product of \(a\) and \(b\) is a terminal cone over the discrete diagram on two objects), and adjunctions generalise the pattern, \(\mathscr{D}(FA,B)\cong\mathscr{C}(A,GB)\) naturally in \(A\) and \(B\). The natural numbers object is the example that matters most here: \(1\xrightarrow{0}N\xrightarrow{s}N\) is such that for every \(1\xrightarrow{x}X\xrightarrow{t}X\) there is a unique \(h\) with \(h\circ 0=x\) and \(h\circ s=t\circ h\),

so that \(N\) is not a set of aggregated elements but a universal iterator, iteration occurring exclusively through composition, beginning at the global element \(0\) and applying the successor endomorphism. Ernie’s and Johnny’s encodings are then simply two natural numbers objects in \(\mathbf{Set}\), and are therefore uniquely isomorphic as structures: the arbitrariness Benacerraf diagnosed is circumvented. The Yoneda lemma is the terminus of this logic. \(\mathrm{Nat}(\mathscr{C}(-,a),P)\cong P(a)\), so the Yoneda embedding \(y:\mathscr{C}\to[\mathscr{C}^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbf{Set}]\) is fully faithful and \[ \mathscr{C}(-,a)\cong\mathscr{C}(-,b)\iff a\cong b. \] The presheaf \(ya=\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathscr{C}}(-,a)\) abstracts from the morphisms of the category those having \(a\) as codomain, together with their behaviour under precomposition, and the corollary says that this relational profile individuates \(a\) up to isomorphism. This formalises and complements Awodey’s thesis, and corresponds to what Assadian and Fraser call relational individuation: the Yoneda lemma is precisely a form of it. [PDF, Ch. 2 §I.1]

Is individuation up to isomorphism enough to establish identity? The answer is a categorical no, if identity is taken literally. The lemma determines an object up to isomorphism by its relational profile, but \(y(a)\cong y(b)\iff a\cong_{\mathscr{C}}b\) does not, in ordinary category theory, entail \(a=b\), which is a much stronger claim. To see why, one must distinguish two uses of category theory. As a language—developed within set theory, with size handled by classes or Grothendieck universes—it offers a partial yes to the demand for unity. The yes is owed to its attention to the network of relations rather than to objects; the partiality to the fact that such categories are built over a set-theoretic background, so that their objects form a set or class and ‘\(a=b\)’ remains the strict equality of underlying sets, which is not invariant under equivalence of categories. Invariance under equivalence is thus a methodological norm of practice, the so-called principle of equivalence, and not a theorem of the framework, which does not prevent one from formulating ‘evil’ statements. Categorical language is chiefly a unifying descriptive grammar: a considerable advance over the purely set-theoretic framework, which still leaves the dialectical movement of structures unexplained. [PDF, Ch. 3 §II.2]

As a foundation—in Lawvere’s Elementary Theory of the Category of Sets and, more generally, in elementary and Grothendieck toposes, whose foundational significance owes much to Lawvere and Tierney and to Grothendieck’s sheaf-theoretic work in algebraic geometry—category theory offers a yes considerably less partial. The logic of a topos is not an additional layer of axioms but is determined by the structure. A subobject classifier \(\Omega\) with \(\mathrm{true}:1\to\Omega\) represents subobjects, \(\mathrm{Sub}(A)\cong\mathrm{Hom}(A,\Omega)\) naturally in \(A\), each mono \(m:A'\rightarrowtail A\) being the pullback of \(\mathrm{true}\) along a unique characteristic map \(\chi_m\):

Each \(\mathrm{Sub}(A)\) is a Heyting algebra, and for \(f:A\to B\) the quantifiers are the adjoints of pullback, \(\exists_f\dashv f^*\dashv\forall_f\). I read Lawvere’s characterisation of quantifiers as adjoints as a prime realisation of objectless structuralism: the logical constants need not be posited externally as linguistic primitives, but arise internally as universal solutions to the structural operation of pulling back predicates—the exact analogue of the \(\Sigma\)- and \(\Pi\)-types of HoTT. This is the opposite of ZFC, where first-order logic is presupposed as independent background (though the definition of an elementary topos is itself still stated in a first-order metatheory, so the autonomy gained is internal rather than absolute). Nor need the logic be classical. In the topos \(\mathrm{Sh}(X)\), \(\Omega(U)\) is the lattice of open subsets of \(U\) and \(\neg V=\mathrm{int}(X\setminus V)\); taking \(X=\mathbb{R}\) and the dense open \(U=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\), one has \(\neg U=\mathrm{int}(\{0\})=\varnothing\), hence \(U\vee\neg U=U\neq\mathbb{R}\), and excluded middle fails, while \(\neg\neg U=\mathbb{R}\). Likewise the topos of \(G\)-sets is Boolean if and only if the monoid \(G\) is a group. Add classifying toposes, \(\mathbf{Geom}(\mathcal{E},\mathbf{Set}[\mathbb{T}])\simeq\mathbb{T}\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}(\mathcal{E})\), and Morita equivalences as Caramello’s ‘bridges’, and theories and their models can be translated across mathematical universes. Here, for the first time, a foundation aligns with the Cavaillèsian conception: it captures a structural becoming and lets logical intelligibility spring from the structure’s own operations. [PDF, Ch. 2 §I.2]

The reservation concerns two limitations that persist in 1-toposes. The first is dimensional truncation: a 1-category has no morphisms between morphisms, and for parallel \(f,g:a\to b\) the assertion \(f=g\) is a mere proposition, so no higher coherence data are retained—moduli problems whose objects have non-trivial automorphisms, for instance, are stacks, not sheaves of sets, and so not objects of a 1-topos. The second is the gap between equivalence and identity: objects remain discrete and strictly equal or not, while structural equivalence stays external to equality; within an ordinary topos, the aporia left by the Yoneda lemma is simply reproduced. The first limitation unfolds into a further problem. The plasticity of topos theory risks dissolving the unity of mathematics into a plurality of local universes, and the network that stitches them together—geometric morphisms and transformations, Morita equivalences—forms the 2-category \(\mathbf{Topos}\), which is not an object of any topos; recovering unity in Cavaillès’s sense would require higher toposes, left exact localisations \(L\dashv i\) of \(\infty\)-presheaf categories \(\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{C})=\mathrm{Fun}(\mathscr{C}^{\mathrm{op}},\mathcal{S})\), which can internalise that mediating network. Finally, intelligibility in a topos depends on further constitutive gestures. A natural numbers object must be postulated, the categorical equivalent of the Axiom of Infinity; an elementary topos is in general only intuitionistic, classical logic being recovered either by stipulating well-pointedness and choice, as in ETCS, or by retreating to the double-negation subtopos \(\mathrm{Sh}_{\neg\neg}(\mathcal{E})\), the smallest dense subtopos, which is Boolean; and the Cauchy and Dedekind reals coincide only under an additional principle such as excluded middle. Such successive rearticulations are effected, within a 1-topos, by external postulations and cuts, which precludes an immanent account of how mathematics’ own network of relations transforms itself. Both limitations, moreover, point to the same place: what is missing is a notion of identity that is itself structured, and a setting in which relations between relations are retained rather than truncated. [PDF, Ch. 3 §II.2]

IV. Paths: Identity with Thickness

Homotopy Type Theory, drawing on Martin-Löf’s dependent type theory and the homotopical interpretation of identity types, addresses precisely this gap. Under propositions-as-types, logic and ontology are treated within one calculus of judgements: objects are constructed and theorems proved by the same rules, the \(\Pi\)-type reading as the universal quantifier and the \(\Sigma\)-type as the existential. More decisively, the intensional identity type \(x=_A y\), introduced by formation rules and the eliminator \(J\), ceases to be a static predicate. By the results of Hofmann–Streicher and of Lumsdaine and van den Berg–Garner, every type carries the structure of a weak \(\infty\)-groupoid: paths \(p:x=y\) compose and invert, associators \((p\cdot q)\cdot r=p\cdot(q\cdot r)\) are themselves paths between paths, and so on indefinitely. The higher structure is not decorative; the Eckmann–Hilton argument shows that for 2-loops \(\alpha,\beta:\Omega^2(A,a)\) one has \(\alpha\cdot\beta=\beta\cdot\alpha\), the type-theoretic form of the commutativity of \(\pi_2\), and functions act as functors on this structure, \(\mathrm{ap}_f(p\cdot q)=\mathrm{ap}_f(p)\cdot\mathrm{ap}_f(q)\). The tower is stratified by homotopy levels, \[ \mathrm{isContr}(A):=\textstyle\sum_{a:A}\prod_{x:A}(a=x),\qquad \text{is-}(n{+}1)\text{-type}(A):=\textstyle\prod_{x,y:A}\text{is-}n\text{-type}(x=_A y), \] and this resolves the dimensional truncation of 1-topos foundations, since transformations between identifications are retained as paths of higher dimension. [PDF, Ch. 2 §II.1]

Axiom (Univalence). For any universe \(\mathcal{U}\) and types \(A,B:\mathcal{U}\), the canonical map \(\mathrm{idtoeqv}_{A,B}:(A=_{\mathcal{U}}B)\to(A\simeq B)\) is an equivalence; its inverse is \(\mathrm{ua}_{A,B}\), whence \[ (A=_{\mathcal{U}}B)\simeq(A\simeq B). \] For categories, analogously, a precategory is univalent when \(\mathrm{idtoiso}_{a,b}:(a=b)\to(a\cong b)\) is an equivalence for all objects \(a,b\).

¶ Voevodsky’s axiom closes the second gap by collapsing propositional equality, formerly left to metatheory, into structural equivalence. An elementary example shows its homotopical content: the Boolean type \(\mathbf{2}\) has an equivalence \(\mathrm{swap}_{\mathbf{2}}\) interchanging \(0_{\mathbf{2}}\) and \(1_{\mathbf{2}}\), and univalence yields a path \(\mathrm{ua}(\mathrm{swap}_{\mathbf{2}}):\mathbf{2}=_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbf{2}\) distinct from \(\mathrm{refl}_{\mathbf{2}}\), since its image under \(\mathrm{idtoeqv}\) is the swap rather than the identity. Even the Boolean type has non-trivial paths in its universe, arising from its automorphisms; identity is no longer a bare coincidence but a structured equivalence along which constructions and proofs may be transported. For every family \(P:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}\), transport gives \((A\simeq B)\to P(A)\to P(B)\), so that every property expressible in the theory is invariant under equivalence. Benacerraf’s problem is thereby dissolved at its root: a statement such as ‘\(1\in 3\)’ has no counterpart for an arbitrary type, because membership is not a structure-invariant notion, and a property that is not invariant is not forbidden, as in categorical practice, but simply not expressible. [PDF, Ch. 3 §II.3]

The same principle reaches categorical structure through univalent categories. Precategories are defined with hom-types that are sets—the small sets of a universe, the small groups with their homomorphisms, the discrete precategory on a set—and a precategory is a (univalent) category when \(\mathrm{idtoiso}\) is an equivalence. If \(\mathcal{U}\) is univalent, \(\mathbf{Set}_{\mathcal{U}}\) is a univalent category, since \((A=B)\simeq(A\simeq B)\simeq(A\cong_{\mathbf{Set}_{\mathcal{U}}}B)\), the first equivalence being univalence and the second identifying equivalences with isomorphisms; and the Structure Identity Principle extends this to standard algebraic structures, so that for groups equality of structured objects corresponds to isomorphism of the underlying structures. The forgetful functor \(U:\mathbf{Grp}\to\mathbf{Set}\), faithful but generally not full, is accordingly not an equivalence. Now combine the Yoneda lemma, restated for precategories, with univalence. Let \(\mathcal{A}\) be univalent and \(a,b:A_0\). The embedding is fully faithful and reflects isomorphisms, and univalence turns isomorphisms into identifications: \[ \hom_{\mathcal{A}}(-,a)\cong\hom_{\mathcal{A}}(-,b)\;\Longrightarrow\;a\cong b\;\Longrightarrow\;a=_{A_0}b. \] Two objects with isomorphic relational profiles are identical. The Rezk completion extends the result to presentations that are not univalent, since every precategory is weakly equivalent to a univalent category and functors out of it extend uniquely up to isomorphism; relational determination is thus internalised in the foundation, independently of presentation. The chain amounts to a structural form of Leibniz’s identity of indiscernibles, indiscernibility being taken relative to the relations that objects bear to all others; and its converse, the indiscernibility of identicals, holds by transport, since an identification \(a=b\) gives \(Pa\simeq Pb\) for every \(P:A_0\to\mathcal{U}\). It is worth pausing on the distance travelled since §I. The Leibnizian identity rested on a complete concept whose infinite analysis only an infinite intellect could complete; here identity is decided by a relational profile that the theory itself states and handles. [PDF, Ch. 2 §II.2]

The framework has, nevertheless, an important limitation, and it reopens the question of dimension. The hom-types of a precategory are sets, and so are the types of isomorphisms, which by univalence are the identity types of objects; the type of objects of a univalent category is therefore a 1-type. Self-identity may carry non-trivial automorphisms, the loops \(a=a\) forming a group, but nothing lies above them, whereas the identity types of an arbitrary type carry paths between paths in every dimension. The categories that HoTT defines internally by these means are one-dimensional fragments of the structure the theory itself possesses; to reach the remainder, hom-sets must give way to the mapping spaces of \((\infty,1)\)-categories, with coherence conditions in every dimension. Relational individuation can therefore be examined at two levels: through the determination of objects by categorical structure, and through the higher homotopical structure of the identifications that relate objects and morphisms. A subtle inflection is required at the second level. Every type admits an interpretation as a weak \(\infty\)-groupoid, yet its homotopical structure may be truncated at any finite level \(n\); the claim is not that every mathematical object carries non-trivial relations in all dimensions, but that the theory has the formal capacity to treat such higher identifications rigorously should they occur. [PDF, Ch. 2 §III]

They do occur, and higher gauge theory shows where. Ordinary gauge fields, 1-forms \(A\), are related by transformations \(A'=g^{-1}Ag+g^{-1}dg\). An Abelian gauge field \(B\), locally a 2-form and geometrically the curvature of a gerbe with connection, transforms in its simplest local formulation as \(B\mapsto B+d\rho_1\), with \(\rho_1\) a 1-form; but \(\rho_1\) and \(\rho_1+d\rho_0\) induce exactly the same transformation, since \(d^2=0\). There is thus a redundancy in the gauge transformations themselves—transformations between transformations, the gauge-of-gauge transformations—which in the complete geometric description also act on the local transition data. The resulting configuration space cannot be adequately represented by a set, nor, if the full hierarchy of equivalences is to be kept, by an ordinary groupoid: it requires in its most elementary form a 2-groupoid, and a field of degree \(p\) gives, under appropriate conditions, a groupoid of order \(p\). Each finite degree yields a finite structure, but the justified possibility of arbitrarily high degrees illustrates the unbounded hierarchy formally accommodated within an \(\infty\)-groupoid. An internalised identity also requires an internal semantics, which a 1-topos cannot provide. Shulman’s theorem gives every Grothendieck \((\infty,1)\)-topos a presentation with strict univalent universes, so that HoTT is interpreted in all of them, and HoTT is expected to serve as the internal language of \(\infty\)-toposes—a claim established, to my knowledge, only in partial form. Univalent syntax above, higher-sheaf semantics below: this dual framework is what makes plausible the dissolution of the mathematical object as a ‘substance’. [PDF, Ch. 2 §III]

For if an object is determined exhaustively by its equivalences in all dimensions, the notion of a fundamental atom or ‘matter’ beneath it disappears, and nothing remains but the network of relations that constitutes it. This draws a sharp line between the substantialist—or, in the term Awodey introduced and Shulman took up, ‘material’—ontologies, which seek an ultimate self-identical substrate, and a structuralist ontology in which being is being-in-relation. With due caution, the essay draws a parallel with eliminative Ontic Structural Realism, which denies individuals intrinsic, self-subsistent identity: \[ \mathcal{S}\prec_{\mathrm{ont}}x_i,\qquad \mathrm{Id}(x_i)\equiv\mathrm{Pos}_{\mathcal{S}}(x_i),\qquad \neg\,\mathrm{Prim}(x_i), \] structure being ontologically prior, identity being position in the structure, and the object being no primitive entity. Gauge theory offers a didactic correspondence: if \(A\sim A^g\) for \(g\in\mathcal{G}\), gauge-invariant observables satisfy \(\mathcal{O}(A)=\mathcal{O}(A^g)\), so physical content resides in what is invariant across representations, much as arithmetical content does across Ernie’s and Johnny’s encodings; and in a Grothendieck topos \(\mathcal{E}=\mathrm{Sh}(\mathscr{C},J)\), objects are determined by their representables while sheaf gluing composes local descriptions into a coherent global organisation preserved under equivalence \(\mathcal{E}\simeq\mathcal{F}\). I call the resulting position a Categorially Invariant Structural Ontology. Two caveats delimit it. The dissolution in question is not the disappearance of objects as formal elements, but the dissolution of their claim to fundamental ontological autonomy. And the passage from mathematical invariance to the ontological priority of structure is an interpretive thesis of structural realism, not a result of category theory or topos theory—one which, in fact, matters little for the argument about mathesis. What remains is the weaker and firmer claim: structure is not what objects possess, but that by virtue of which they can be determined as objects.

We can now return HoTT’s answer to the criterion of §I. Because it does not reduce mathematics to a static substrate of basic objects, and because transformations, equivalences and higher coherences function within it as generative principles of intelligibility, it is the most nuanced of the three responses. It is neither a definitive closure of the foundational question nor a literal realisation of the early-modern mathesis. Size paradoxes still require a hierarchy of universes \(\mathcal{U}_0:\mathcal{U}_1:\cdots\); the standard semantics in Kan complexes is carried out within ZFC with inaccessible cardinals, so its foundational autonomy remains subject to metatheoretical scrutiny; excluded middle and the equivalence of Dedekind and Cauchy reals remain delicate constructive choices; only the \((-1)\)-truncated types behave as propositions in the classical sense; and judgemental equality \(\equiv\) remains external to the theory. What HoTT offers is a framework in which the unity of mathematics is understood as an open, invariant and self-transforming network of relations. The comparison can be summarised:

V. Mathesis Reinterpreted

¶ The comparison has so far relied on an informal notion of unity, and the history in §I did not supply a formal one. The authors associated with mathesis proved of little use here, nor did Bacon or van Roomen: what they offer is aspiration without rigour, a fault of historical context rather than of the authors, since the mathematics they knew is a completely different beast from that of higher toposes and homotopy type theory. A definition had therefore to be constructed, in a strong and a weak version.

Definition (Strong and Weak Mathematical Unity). Mathematical Unity obtains in a mathematical system \(\mathfrak{M}\), relative to a foundation \(\mathbb{F}\), if and only if its components and constitutive relations can be formally constructed and articulated from a common conceptual foundation, through a unified set of formation principles and rules that preserves their structural, operational and inferential organisation: \[ \mathrm{MU}_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathfrak{M})\iff\exists E_{\mathbb{F}}\,\big[\,\mathrm{Gen}_{\mathbb{F}}(E_{\mathbb{F}},\mathfrak{M})\wedge\mathrm{Pres}_{\mathbb{F}}(E_{\mathbb{F}},\mathfrak{M})\,\big], \] where \(\mathrm{Gen}_{\mathbb{F}}\) requires \(\forall c\in C(\mathfrak{M})\,\mathrm{Build}_{\mathbb{F}}(E_{\mathbb{F}}(c))\) and \(\mathrm{Pres}_{\mathbb{F}}\) requires \(\forall r\in\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{M})\,\mathrm{Preserve}_{\mathbb{F}}(E_{\mathbb{F}},r)\). Weak unity, \(\mathrm{WMU}_{\mathbb{F}}(E,\mathfrak{M})\iff\mathrm{WGen}_{\mathbb{F}}(E,\mathfrak{M})\wedge\mathrm{WPres}_{\mathbb{F}}(E,\mathfrak{M})\), restricts the representation map \(E\) to proper parts, \(C_E\subsetneq C(\mathfrak{M})\), \(\mathcal{R}_E\subsetneq\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{M})\), with the gradient \[ E_1\preceq E_2\iff C_{E_1}\subseteq C_{E_2}\wedge\mathcal{R}_{E_1}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{E_2}. \]

The definition only became clear once worked through examples, and they show how it operates. Take \(\mathfrak{M}=(\mathbb{N},+,0)\) with HoTT as foundation. One defines the type \(\mathbb{N}_{\mathbb{H}}:\mathcal{U}\) with \(0:\mathbb{N}_{\mathbb{H}}\) and \(S:\mathbb{N}_{\mathbb{H}}\to\mathbb{N}_{\mathbb{H}}\); numbers are its elements, addition is a function \(+_{\mathbb{H}}:\mathbb{N}_{\mathbb{H}}\to\mathbb{N}_{\mathbb{H}}\to\mathbb{N}_{\mathbb{H}}\), equality is the identity type, and associativity is the type \(\prod_{a,b,c:\mathbb{N}_{\mathbb{H}}}\big((a+b)+c=a+(b+c)\big)\), whose proof is a term inhabiting it. Packaging the whole as a monoid, \[ \mathrm{Monoid}_{\mathbb{H}}=\textstyle\sum_{A:\mathcal{U}}\sum_{\mu:A\to A\to A}\sum_{e:A}\big(\mathrm{Assoc}(\mu)\times\mathrm{LeftId}(\mu,e)\times\mathrm{RightId}(\mu,e)\big), \] one obtains \(m_{\mathbb{H}}:=(\mathbb{N}_{\mathbb{H}},0,+,p_{\mathrm{assoc}},p_{\mathrm{left}},p_{\mathrm{right}}):\mathrm{Monoid}_{\mathbb{H}}\), so \(\mathrm{Gen}_{\mathbb{H}}\) holds; and the injective representation \(\rho:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}_{\mathbb{H}}\) satisfies \(\rho(0)=0\) and \(\rho(m+n)=\rho(m)+_{\mathbb{H}}\rho(n)\), with associativity and the identity laws carried over, so \(\mathrm{Pres}_{\mathbb{H}}\) holds and \(\mathfrak{M}\) is unified relative to HoTT. A category \(\mathfrak{C}=(\mathrm{ob},\mathrm{Hom},\mathrm{id},\circ)\) goes the same way, with \(\mathrm{Ob}:\mathcal{U}\), \(\mathrm{Hom}:\mathrm{Ob}\to\mathrm{Ob}\to\mathcal{U}\), \(\mathrm{id}:\prod_{A}\mathrm{Hom}(A,A)\), composition \(\prod_{A,B,C}\mathrm{Hom}(B,C)\to\mathrm{Hom}(A,B)\to\mathrm{Hom}(A,C)\), and associativity as the type \(\prod_{A,B,C,D}\prod_{f,g,h}\big((h\circ g)\circ f=h\circ(g\circ f)\big)\). The weak notion is illustrated in ZFC. Let \(\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{num}}\) comprise \(\mathbb{N},\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{R}\) with their algebraic and order relations, and consider the fragment \(\mathfrak{M}'=\{\mathbb{N},\mathbb{Z}\}\) under addition, with \(\mathbb{N}=\omega\), \(\mathbb{Z}=(\omega\times\omega)/\!\sim\), \((a,b)\sim(c,d)\iff a+d=b+c\), and \(\iota:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Z}\), \(\iota(n)=[(n,0)]\). Then \(\iota(0_{\mathbb{N}})=[(0,0)]=0_{\mathbb{Z}}\), \(\iota(m+n)=[(m+n,0)]=\iota(m)+_{\mathbb{Z}}\iota(n)\), and \(\iota(m)=\iota(n)\iff m+0=n+0\iff m=n\): an injective homomorphism from the additive monoid of the naturals to the additive group of the integers, so the selected relations are preserved and \(\mathfrak{M}'\) is partially unified under ZFC. [PDF, Ch. 3 §III.1]

The examples also show what the definition does not do: it states a necessary condition, satisfied by ZFC as well as by HoTT, and it is the Cavaillèsian criterion of §§II–IV, not the definition alone, that discriminates between foundations. The second function of mathesis—mathematics as a measure of the scientificity of other disciplines—is more delicate, since in the language of HoTT it amounts to unifying several distinct semantic universes. The history again supplies two candidates, neither adopted. Descartes’s Regulae transpose to other disciplines not the content of mathematics but its method, so that the question is not how mathematisable a discipline is, but how far it can treat its objects in a clear, orderly and measurable manner—measurable in the sense of being placed in relation to others of the same nature by a common criterion of comparability—so that reason may decompose the problem. Leibniz, after linking the mathesis in early texts to quantity, broadens it in the Elementa nova matheseos universalis into a ‘logic of imagination’ that should supply the method by which conceivable things are determined with exactness, and the characteristica goes further still, projecting an irrefutable calculus even for morals and metaphysics. Gluing of schemes gives a mathematical image of this passage from simple to complex: the projective line \(\mathbb{P}^1_k\) is obtained from two copies of \(\mathbb{A}^1_k\) by identifying their non-vanishing open subsets through \(s=t^{-1}\), a global structure built from simpler local objects. The Leibnizian yardstick thus demands decomposability into simpler, rigorously determined concepts, univocal expression by well-determined symbols, and inference by formal rules verifiable through the manipulation of formulae. I retain from both the absence of any need to mathematise a discipline, but adopt a structuralist definition instead.

Definition (Gradient of Scientificity). The Scientificity Scale is an epistemological structure of explicit criteria, rationally justifiable and intersubjectively assessable, by means of which the degree of consistency, rigour, systematicity and justificatory legitimacy of a system of knowledge is determined, as a function of the nature of its object and of the methods appropriate to its investigation. Formally \(\mathcal{R}=\{C_1,\dots,C_n\}\), and a system \(K\) has the profile \[ \mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{R}}(K):=(c_1(K),\dots,c_n(K)), \] the criteria being (i) conceptual consistency, (ii) systematic coherence, (iii) justification, (iv) controllability, (v) intersubjectivity and (vi) corrigibility.

The definition does not attribute scientificity as a status; it is an instrument for investigating the possibility of a mathesis, and its criteria must be adapted to each discipline, since even close disciplines differ considerably. Physical cosmology and pseudo-Riemannian geometry make the point. Cosmology rests on General Relativity, whose spacetime is a Lorentzian manifold \((M,g)\) of signature \((-,+,+,+)\), with its Levi-Civita connection and curvature, coupled to matter by \(G+\Lambda g=8\pi G_{\mathrm N}T\), \(G:=\mathrm{Ric}-\mathrm{scal}\,g\), whose left-hand side is purely geometric; homogeneity and isotropy single out the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric \(g=-dt^2+a(t)^2\gamma_{ij}dx^idx^j\), reducing Einstein’s equations to \[ H^2:=\Big(\frac{\dot a}{a}\Big)^2=\frac{8\pi G_{\mathrm N}}{3}\rho-\frac{k}{a^2}+\frac{\Lambda}{3}. \] The formalism lives largely in geometry; the modes of inference do not. Mathematics unfolds, with Cavaillès, by the dialectical becoming of its internal structure, whereas cosmology, an empirical science, depends on agents external to its formalism. The background solution says nothing about inhomogeneities: one perturbs \(g=\bar g+h\), and the geometry does not fix the statistics of \(h\), which enter as initial conditions to be confronted with observation. Treating the density contrast as a homogeneous, isotropic random field, its power spectrum is defined by \(\langle\delta(\mathbf{k})\delta^*(\mathbf{k}')\rangle=(2\pi)^3\delta_D(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}')P(k)\), and for the anisotropies of the cosmic microwave background, \(\langle a_{\ell m}a^*_{\ell'm'}\rangle=\delta_{\ell\ell'}\delta_{mm'}C_\ell\). The theory predicts \(C_\ell\) from a few parameters, but observation provides a single sky, hence only the estimator \(\hat C_\ell=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_m|a_{\ell m}|^2\), subject to an irreducible cosmic variance \(\Delta C_\ell/C_\ell=\sqrt{2/(2\ell+1)}\). The chain geometry \(\to\) Einstein equations \(\to\) Friedmann equation \(\to\) linear perturbations is internal and deductive on the side of mathematics; the passage to \(P(k)\), to \(C_\ell\) and to the observed sky is statistical and external on the side of physics. If two fields so close in formalism differ so much in inferential regime, the situation is more delicate still for disciplines as heterogeneous as mathematics and the social sciences. A second set of criteria assesses the foundations themselves. With \(\mathcal{F}=\{F_{\mathrm{ZFC}},F_{\mathrm{Cat}},F_{\mathrm{HoTT}}\}\) and \(\mathcal{D}\) the scientific domains whose structures bear on the universal scope of mathematics, an assessment \(\mathfrak{A}:\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{V}^n\) assigns to each pair the profile \(\big(C(F),E(F,d),S(F,d),I(F,d),V(F),T(F),U(F,d)\big)\)—logical and metatheoretical coherence, expressive adequacy, structural fidelity, interfoundational interoperability, formal verifiability, epistemological transparency, unificatory capacity—each scored on the ordinal scale \(\mathcal{V}=\{0,1,2\}\) (insufficient, partially adequate, adequate), every value requiring a mathematical or epistemological justification. A system is admissible, \(\mathrm{Adm}(F)\), when it offers sufficient grounds for the coherence, justification and examination of its principles, and a strong candidate for the role of mathesis must satisfy \(\mathrm{MU}(F,\mathcal{D})\iff\mathrm{Adm}(F)\wedge\bigwedge_{d\in\mathcal{D}}[E(F,d)=S(F,d)=U(F,d)=2]\). The aim is not an a priori hierarchy of foundations, nor the equation of rigour with universal applicability, but simply to determine whether any system, or a principled articulation of several, can meet the requirements of the universal ambition. The comparative matrix records, accordingly, characteristic features rather than predetermined scores: set-theoretic representation, encoding of structures and broad classical coverage for ZFC; relational unity, preservation through morphisms and universal properties for categories; identity, equivalence and higher structure, with an intrinsic connection between proofs, types and verification, for HoTT. All three lines of argument—the definition of unity, the gradient of scientificity and the assessment of foundations—now converge, under the two philosophical constraints recovered in §I: Kant’s limitation and Cavaillès’s mathematical becoming.

Definition and Thesis (Mathesis Universalis Reinterpreted). Mathesis universalis is a universal mathematical framework grounded in a structural conception of mathematical unity and erected upon Homotopy Type Theory as its primary foundational architecture. It furnishes an integrative foundation for mathematical knowledge and a regulative structure of formal and epistemological criteria for assessing the structural coherence, formal expressibility and justificatory legitimacy of mathematical representations and models across scientific domains. For a configuration \(\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\), \[ \mathrm{MU}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})\iff\bigwedge_{F\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{Adm}(F)\;\wedge\;\bigwedge_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\Big[\exists F\in\mathcal{C}\,\big(E(F,d)=S(F,d)=U(F,d)=2\big)\wedge\Big(|\mathcal{C}|>1\Rightarrow\bigwedge_{F\in\mathcal{C}}I(F,d)=2\Big)\Big], \] which reduces to the single-system condition when \(\mathcal{C}=\{F\}\); and the central claim is \[ \mathrm{MU}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})\;\Longrightarrow\;F_{\mathrm{HoTT}}\in\mathcal{C}, \] each \(F\in\mathcal{C}\) admitting an interpretation \(\iota_F:F\to F_{\mathrm{HoTT}}\) that preserves the structural, operational and inferential organisation of its components:

¶ The thesis does not exclude the other systems; it places HoTT as the architecture through which their structures and mutual relations are articulated and assessed. It holds together four demands that emerged along the way. First, it preserves the classical aspiration to the unity of mathematical knowledge—the integration of heterogeneous disciplines within a coherent order—not as an ontological reduction, which is exactly what §II found wanting in the set-theoretic answer. Second, it recovers the Leibnizian interplay of characteristica and calculus: in HoTT structures, propositions and their relations are expressed by types and dependent types, and propositions-as-types makes proofs themselves formal objects, a proposition \(P\) being established when a term \(p:P\) is exhibited by explicit rules, so that universal representation and systematic deduction are joined. Third, it absorbs the Kantian restriction: formalisation is not assumed to extend without limit, mathematical representation does not of itself confer empirical validity or legitimacy, and the limits of applicability are written into the definition. Fourth, it honours Cavaillès: unity cannot be a closed axiomatic edifice, and the hierarchy of universes \(\mathcal{U}_0:\mathcal{U}_1:\mathcal{U}_2:\cdots\), higher inductive types and the homotopical reading of structure give HoTT an extensible architecture whose foundational role does not require that it bring the development of mathematics to a close. The resulting framework is universal in its capacity for representation, formalisation and assessment, critical in its reach, and open to the historical movement of mathematics.

The third demand is made precise rather than left to good intentions, and it answers the second of the two problems with which the essay began. Nothing here entails the methodological homogenisation of the sciences; the social sciences are not asked to adopt the procedures of mathematical or chemical investigation. To each domain \(d\in\mathcal{D}\) is associated its own family of criteria \(\mathcal{R}_d\), adapted from the gradient of scientificity, so that a system of knowledge \(K\) in \(d\) is assessed by \(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{R}_d}(K)\); and writing \(\mathcal{R}_d=\mathcal{R}_d^{\mathrm{form}}\sqcup\mathcal{R}_d^{\mathrm{emp}}\), where the formal part comprises conceptual consistency, systematic coherence and justification and the remainder belongs to the procedures of the discipline, the role of mathesis is restricted to the projection \[ \pi_{\mathrm{form}}\,\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{R}_d}(K):=\big(c_1(K),c_2(K),c_3(K)\big), \] without collapsing empirical inquiry into formal deduction. The cosmological example shows why the cut is needed: controllability, intersubjectivity and corrigibility, as exercised in the confrontation of \(\hat C_\ell\) with a single sky, are the property of the discipline, not of the formalism. The reinterpreted mathesis universalis thus makes no claim to exhaust scientific validity. It supplies a shared structural language through which distinct fields can be formally articulated and their relations examined, and a common regulative frame for the formal dimensions of knowledge, while leaving the empirical and methodological autonomy of each discipline intact. In this way the two problems of §0 are finally given their proper relation: the first, the unity of mathematics, receives a structural and homotopical answer; the second, the authority of mathematics over knowledge in general, is confined, in Kant’s spirit, to a regulative and formal role.
