Structure, Ontology, and the Quarrel over the Foundations of Mathematics

Published: [YYYY-MM-DD]

Introduction: Reinterpretin Mathesis

Contemporary dispute over the foundations of mathematics, between the set-theoretic orthodoxy of ZFC and the structural alternatives of category theory and homotopy type theory, invites a reconsideration of the early modern idea of a mathesis universalis, that is, of a universal science whose unity may be sought either in the objects it treats or in the relations that order them. This work then asks, as a guiding hypothesis, whether the classical idea may be reinterpreted in the light of the modern debate. The reinterpretation begins at the threshold of modernity, with the dissatisfaction from which Descartes' formulation arose. Seeking the necessary method for discovering the truth, Descartes recalls the ancients and questions their rationale for treating a knowledge of mathematics, however rudimentary, as a conditio sine qua non for embarking upon the intellectual life.

Yet, he harboured a profound dissatisfaction with the prevailing state of these disciplines; perceiving the arithmetic and geometry of his time as a disjointed collection of arbitrary rules, he found them lacking in a deeper methodological unity. Consequently, he abstracts away from the vulgar mathematics of his epoch — comprising rules applied to those “empty problems with which arithmeticians and geometers are wont to amuse themselves” — and recovers a mathematics that contains “the primary rudiments of human reason” and which, as a discipline, is capable of extending “to the eliciting of true results on every subject” [Descartes, 1985]. That is to say, Descartes arrives at a mathematics which, as a method, is in a certain sense more general than the vulgar one, concerning itself with whatever can be investigated insofar as it involves order and measure, independently of whether its objects are numbers, geometric figures, stars, sounds, or other things. Furthermore, as a discipline, it proves superior in utility and facility to the other mathematical sciences — such as astronomy, optics, and mechanics — by subordinating their particular investigations to the more general examination of order and measure. This subordination is evident in the fact that it encompasses the same objects as these particular sciences, while extending beyond them to many others. It is this foundational science1 that Descartes terms Mathesis Universalis — or, Universal Mathematics. However, as noted by Rabouin in [Rabouin, 2009], the concept of mathesis universalis appears before Descartes within philosophical reflections on the unity of mathematics, rather than the mathematization of nature. This concept is explicitly linked — primarily by Descartes himself — to a long tradition of problems. It does not accompany a claim of a conceptual rupture, however. Instead, this reflection is situated within the heritage of the Ancients, above all Proclus, whose discussion of the problem develops in opposition to a solution previously proposed by Aristotle.

Such a Cartesian concept of mathesis universalis, embedded in the geometrical concepts of his time (despite his critique of their lack of a general method, as outlined), constitutes a first structural intellectualisation of extension — that is, of “whatever has length, breadth and depth” [Descartes, 1985] —, and thus remained undisturbed for a considerable period of time. Yet the advent of the differential calculus in the seventeenth century brought a crisis into the concept of the continuum. Beyond the appearance of infinitesimal quantities, mathematical determination came to depend upon relations between quantities in variation. In the (Leibnizian) expression for a derivative

\[\frac{dy}{dx},\]

neither \(dy\) nor \(dx\) possesses an independent determination sufficient for the mathematical operations in which they figure, it is unsurprising that Newton was compelled to conceptualise these quantities dynamically, designating them as `fluxions' and `fluents' [Newton, 1687]. The continuum could no longer be exhausted by determinate extension and magnitude, and had to be apprehended through variation, dependence, and limiting relations. This compelled the post-Cartesian reconsideration of Descartes' methodological project. Driven by this shift, Leibniz sought to extend the universality of mathesis beyond the domain of magnitude, connecting it with a general logic of relations. The universal was consequently displaced from quantities, that is, from measurement, towards the formal relations through which terms are necessarily connected. In his correspondence with Arnauld, Leibniz writes that “there must always be some basis for the connection between the terms of a proposition, and it is to be found in their concepts” [Leibniz, 1686]. In this sense, mathesis universalis is transfigured into an ars combinatoria, in which the intelligibility of a system resides in the necessary relational structure through which its terms are constituted.

Later, this conception of mathesis embraced by Leibniz was construed [Husserl, 1936; Koyré, 1939; Foucault, 1966] as an idea of mathematising nature; nevertheless, Foucault's archaeology of the classical épistémè retrospectively clarifies the significance of this transfiguration. He diagnoses not only the reordering of the relation between measure and order, such that problems of measure can ultimately be reduced to problems of order, but also clarifies that mathesis universalis, understood as a general science of order and measure, was never primarily aimed at the mathematization of nature [Foucault, 1966]:

Mais ni cet effort ni les essais du mécanisme ne doivent être confondus avec le rapport que tout le savoir classique, en sa forme la plus générale, entretient avec la mathesis, entendue comme science universelle de la mesure et de l'ordre. Sous les mots vides, obscurément magiques, d'« influence cartésienne » ou de « modèle newtonien », les historiens des idées ont l'habitude de mêler ces trois choses, et de définir le rationalisme classique par la tentation de rendre la nature mécanique et calculable. Les autres — les demi-habiles — s'efforcent de découvrir sous ce rationalisme le jeu de « forces contraires » : celles d'une nature et d'une vie qui ne se laissent réduire ni à l'algèbre ni à la physique du mouvement et qui maintiennent ainsi, au fond du classicisme, la ressource du non rationalisable. Ces deux formes d'analyse sont aussi insuffisantes l'une que l'autre. Car le fondamental, pour l'épistémè classique, ce n'est ni le succès ou l'échec du mécanisme, ni le droit ou l'impossibilité de mathématiser la nature, mais bien un rapport à la mathesis qui, jusqu'à la fin du XVIIIe siècle, demeure constant et inaltéré. Ce rapport présente deux caractères essentiels. Le premier c'est que les relations entre les êtres seront bien pensées sous la forme de l'ordre et de la mesure, mais avec ce déséquilibre fondamental qu'on peut toujours ramener les problèmes de la mesure à ceux de l'ordre.

Cavaillès later formulates the same insight in the words “Le lien nécessaire aperçu entre deux termes garantit l'ensemble” [Cavaillès, 1947], presenting the extension of a system through iteration and combination as at once a source of novelty and a guarantee of intelligibility. In this sense, the Leibnizian transformation that Foucault retrospectively identifies as characteristic of the classical mathesis receives, in Cavaillès, a more explicitly structural and dynamic formulation, as what guarantees the unity of a system is not the independent determination of its terms, but the necessary relations through which the system is extended and transformed.

This structuralist character, which Leibniz imparted to the mathesis universalis, nevertheless encountered an obstacle in the very foundations of the discipline that had inspired it. The advent of set theory — grounded in the Zermelo–Fraenkel axioms together with the Axiom of Choice — sought to formalise mathematics and to resolve many of its conceptual difficulties in the early twentieth century. In doing so, it brought to light an extensional ontology that grounded mathematics in a bottom-up framework: simpler and more fundamental objects were defined first, and more complex mathematical objects were then constructed from them. Such an ontology was substantial in essence rather than structural. Here, a number — say, \(n\) — is defined and entirely characterized by its internal constitution, namely

\[n = \left\{ \emptyset,\, \{\emptyset\},\, \{\emptyset,\{\emptyset\}\},\, \ldots,\, \{0,1,\ldots,n-2\} \right\}.\]

where \(\emptyset\) denotes the empty set. For example, for the natural numbers \(0, 1, 2\) and \(3\), one have \(0 = \emptyset\), \(1 = \{\emptyset\} = \{0\}\), \(2 = \{\emptyset, \{\emptyset\}\} = \{0, 1\}\) and \(3 = \{\emptyset, \{\emptyset\}, \{\emptyset, \{\emptyset\}\}\} = \{0,1,2\}\), and so on, ad infinitum, within the von Neumann framework. Consequently, an object is reduced to its extensional composition; its relations to other mathematical objects, or its role within the broader mathematical structure it inhabits or even defines, are rendered strictly secondary to this atomic, bottom-up characterisation. This foundational framework of mathematics remained largely unchallenged for some time, but already in the late nineteenth (with regard to naive set theory) and early twentieth centuries, the paradoxes associated with Cantor [Cantor, 1895], Burali-Forti [Burali-Forti, 1897], Russell [Russell, 1903], and Frege [Frege, 1903] exposed serious difficulties in the unrestricted conception of sets and in the logical principles underlying such foundations. Later, Gödel's incompleteness theorems [Gödel, 1931] revealed fundamental limitations on what sufficiently expressive formal systems can establish about themselves, while Benacerraf [Benacerraf, 1965] raised a different, albeit more important to the present work, philosophical problem concerning the identification and ontology of mathematical objects, arguing that the natural numbers cannot be uniquely identified with any particular set-theoretic construction. The latter reintroduces a structuralist motif to the discussion, as it entails that numbers are merely positions within an abstract structure [Benacerraf, 1965],

[&dots;] numbers are not objects at all, because in giving the properties (that is, necessary and sufficient) of numbers you merely characterize an abstract structure — and the distinction lies in the fact that the "elements" of the structure have no properties other than those relating them to other "elements" of the same structure. That a system of objects exhibits the structure of the integers implies that the elements of that system have some properties not dependent on structure.

These fractures within the set-theoretic foundations of mathematics consequently compelled mathematicians to seek alternatives that would allow them to rigorously maintain and expand the theoretical corpus of the discipline, whilst staunching these newly exposed vulnerabilities. As noted above, Category Theory emerged as one such alternative. Initially proposed by Eilenberg and Mac Lane [Eilenberg & Mac Lane, 1945] to address problems in algebraic topology — or, more precisely, to study natural transformations, as Mac Lane himself famously observed, "Categories have been defined in order to be able to define functors, and functors have been defined in order to be able to define natural transformations" [Mac Lane, 1998]. Over time, Category Theory acquired a degree of autonomy, one that freed it from being perceived as a mere heuristic device or organisational tool within the set-theoretic paradigm. It thereby assumed a central position in the discourse on the foundations of mathematics, a paradigm shift fundamentally driven by the advent of topos theory. While the concept of a topos was initially conceived by Grothendieck for algebraic geometry [Artin, Grothendieck, & Verdier, 1972], it was Lawvere and Tierney who rigorously developed elementary topos theory in the late 1960s and early 1970s [Lawvere, 1970; Tierney, 1972]. By elegantly abstracting the properties of the category of sets, topos theory demonstrated that mathematics could be firmly grounded upon categorical structures and their internal logic — often intuitionistic — thereby offering a robust, self-contained alternative to classical Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory. More recently, Homotopy Type Theory stood as yet another structuralist alternative to the extensional set-theoretic foundations of mathematics. As the name suggests, Homotopy Type Theory combines Type Theory, first introduced by Bertrand Russell [Russell, 1903; Russell, 1908] in order to cope with some contradictions he found in his account of set theory [Coquand, 2013; Univalent Foundations Program, 2013], and with homotopy theory, a branch of algebraic topology that investigates mathematical contexts wherein morphisms are endowed with a concept of equivalent deformations — that is, contexts in which they are equipped with a homotopy \(\kappa\) between them,

a notion which shall be rigorously defined in what follows. Such homotopical interpretation of Type Theory eventually acquired an explicitly foundational ambition with the formulation of the Univalence Axiom and the emergence of the Univalent Foundations programme [Univalent Foundations Program, 2013]. Of greater consequence still, the adoption of Homotopy Type Theory as a foundational framework, together with the Univalence Axiom and its homotopical interpretation of identity, enables the establishment of a genuinely invariant notion of mathematical objects. This implies, notably, that isomorphic mathematical structures may be legitimately identified. Remarkably, such identification constitutes an everyday operative norm for the working mathematician, yet a conceptual transgression against the orthodox set-theoretic foundations.

Ultimately, both Category Theory and Homotopy Type Theory provide formal frameworks through which the relational conception of mathesis universalis, as envisioned by Leibniz and retrospectively diagnosed by Foucault, can be reinterpreted. The historical trajectory of modern mathematics can thus be understood through a fundamental dichotomy. On one side stands the set-theoretic orthodoxy of ZFC, which embodies a `substantial' mathesis — an extensional framework anchoring mathematics in the atomic, bottom-up accumulation of static objects. On the other side stand these modern categorical and homotopical alternatives, which champion a truly `structural' mathesis. Within this relational paradigm, the internal substance of an object is rendered virtually obsolete; mathematical entities are instead exhaustively determined by the network of mappings, equivalences, and transformations (the morphisms) that situate them within a broader structure. This perspective, I argue, provides a contemporary formal setting in which the Leibnizian ambition underlying the characteristica universalis may be reinterpreted, as under such framework, intelligibility is no longer grounded primarily in the independent determination of objects, but in the necessary relations through which they are connected. The present work, then, aims at the possibility of reinterpretation as its guiding hypothesis. It does not assume a historical continuity between the classical mathesis universalis and contemporary structural (or even substantial) foundations of mathematics, however. Instead, it asks whether the emergence of categorical and homotopical foundations may be understood as a reconfiguration of the universalising ambition that underlay the earlier conception of a universal science of order. In this sense, the structural alternatives to set-theoretic foundations are considered not as a recovery of an abandoned philosophical programme — I am, admittedly, unable to sustain such a strong claim — but as a contemporary reformulation of a problem that has accompanied the project of mathematical universality from its earliest modern articulations.

This work is organised in two main parts. The first is devoted to the conception of mathematics as grounded in substance. It begins with a formal introduction to ZFC and an examination of the way in which set theory operates as a foundation for mathematics. It then turns to its relation to the characteristica universalis, before considering the cumulative hierarchy as a foundational construction ultimately grounded in mathematical objects. The second part turns to category theory, initially treating categories in their more usual role as a mathematical theory in their own right. It then introduces the notion of a topos and considers the foundational ambitions of categorical mathematics, followed by a formal introduction to Homotopy Type Theory. The Yoneda Lemma will be discussed throughout these different settings, serving as a recurring point of comparison between their respective conceptions of structure. The discussion then turns to the relativisation of the individual, examining how arrows in category theory and spaces and paths in homotopy theory bear upon the structural conception of mathesis universalis associated here with Leibniz and retrospectively articulated by Foucault. The final subsection, The Archaeology of Order and the Autonomous Dialectic of the Concept, takes this connection in a more exploratory direction, bringing the structural character of category theory and Homotopy Type Theory into conversation with Cavaillès's philosophy of the concept and Foucault's conception of mathesis as an order operating without a subject.

§ I. Mathesis as Structure: Categories, HoTT, and Subjectless Structuralism

§ I.A Formal Framework I: Category Theory and the Yoneda Lemma

¶ 1. The concept of a category can be formalized within two primary theoretical frameworks. The first relies on a purely axiomatic approach, wherein these structures are designated as metacategories. The second framework, which serves a natural extetion — or, more rigorously, a particular case — of the former, represents the standard formulation of categories within Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory with Axiom of Choice (ZFC). We thus commence our exposition by introducing this strictly axiomatic characterization:

Definition 1 (Metagraph). A Metagraph comprises a collection of objects \(a,b,c,\dots\), a collection of arrows \(f,g,h\dots\) and two operations, defined as follows: (i) Domain, which assigns to each arrow \(f\) an object \(a = \operatorname{dom}(f)\); (ii) Codomain, which assigns to each arrow \(f\) an object \(b = \operatorname{cod}(f)\).

Diagrammatically, these operations may be depicted as arrows from \(a\) to \(b\), denoted by \(a \xrightarrow[]{f} b\). Consequently, a finite graph can be conceptualized as a concatenation of such arrows, expressed, for example, as \(a \xrightarrow[]{f} b \xrightarrow[]{g} c \xrightarrow[]{h}\). Equipped with the underlying structure of a metagraph, we can proceed to the definition of a metacategory:

Definition 2 (Metacategory). A \textit{metacategory} is a metagraph equipped with two additional operations, namely: (i) Identity, wich assigns to each object \(a\) an arrow \(a \xrightarrow[]{\mathrm{id}_a} a\); (ii) \textit{Composition}, commonly denoted by \(\circ\) or by \(\cdot\), which assigns to each pair \(\langle g, f \rangle\) of arrows with \(\operatorname{dom} (g) = \operatorname{cod} (f)\) an arrow \(\operatorname{dom} (f) \xrightarrow[]{g \circ f} \operatorname{cod} (g) \), which is called their composite, which is visually represented by the following commutative diagram:
Example 1. The metacategory of sets consists of all sets \(X, Y, \dots\) as objects and all functions between sets, \(f: X \rightarrow Y\), as arrows, equipped with standard function composition.
Example 2. The metacategory of groups consists of all groups \(G, H, \dots\) as objects and all group homomorphisms, \(f: G \rightarrow H\), as arrows, equipped with standard function composition.

Moreover, composition and the assignment of identities to objects are subject to the following axioms, understood to hold whenever the arrows in question are composable:

  • (Associativity): For any composable arrows \(a \xrightarrow{f} b \xrightarrow{g} c \xrightarrow{h} d\), one always has \(h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f\).
  • (Identity Law): For any arrows \(a \xrightarrow{f} b\) and \(b \xrightarrow{g} c\), the identity arrow \(\mathrm{id}_b\) satisfies \(\mathrm{id}_b \circ f = f\) and \(g \circ \mathrm{id}_b = g\).

Formally, a commutative diagram is defined as follows: let \(\Gamma\) be a diagram representing a collection of objects and arrows within a metacategory \(C\). We say that \(\Gamma\) commutes if, for any two objects \(x\) and \(y\) in \(\Gamma\), all directed paths of arrows connecting \(x\) to \(y\) yield the same composite arrow in \(C\). The two diagrams above are instances of this notion: in each, every directed path between a given pair of objects composes to the same arrow.

Furthermore, the identity arrow of an object \(b\) within a metacategory \(C\) is uniquely determined: if \(e\) and \(e'\) both satisfy the identity law for \(b\), then \(e = e \circ e' = e'\), the first equality using \(e\) as a left identity and the second using \(e'\) as a right identity. Consequently, it is common practice to identify this arrow directly with the object itself, establishing the notational equivalence \(b = \mathrm{id}_b\). This identification is licensed by the fact that identity arrows are themselves characterizable in purely arrow-theoretic terms --- as those arrows \(e\) satisfying \(e \circ f = f\) and \(g \circ e = g\) whenever the respective compositions are defined --- and stand in canonical bijective correspondence with the objects of the category. It thereby becomes possible to dispense with objects altogether, defining a category instead as a class of arrows equipped with a partial composition operation subject to the usual associativity and identity axioms, with objects recovered only derivatively, as the identity arrows themselves. The data for an arrows-only metacategory consists of a class of arrows \(A\), together with an operation that assigns to each composable pair \(\langle g, f \rangle\) their composite, namely, the arrow \(g \circ f\). In this context, one defines a unit (which will assume the role of the identity arrow) in \(A\) as follows:

Definition 3 (Unit). Let \(A\) be a class equipped with a partial binary operation of composition. An arrow \(u \in A\) is a unit in \(A\) if
  • \(u \circ u\) is defined and \(u \circ u = u\);
  • for every \(k \in A\), if \(u \circ k\) is defined then \(u \circ k = k\);
  • for every \(k \in A\), if \(k \circ u\) is defined then \(k \circ u = k\).

An arrow \(u\) that already sits inside an objects-and-arrows metacategory as some \(\mathrm{id}_b\) is trivially its own left and right unit. So, to dispense with objects, this absorbing behavior is taken as the definition of the arrows that will play their role. With this established, one can formally define an arrows-only metacategory as follows:

Definition 4 (Arrows-only Metacategory). An arrows-only metacategory is a class \(A\) equipped with a partial binary operation of composition, subject to the following axioms:
  • (Associativity): For all \(f, g, h \in A\), \(h \circ (g \circ f)\) is defined if and only if \(g \circ f\) and \(h \circ g\) are both defined; and in that case, \((h \circ g) \circ f\) is also defined and \(h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f\).
  • (Existence of Units): For every \(f \in A\), there exist units \(u, v\) (Definition 3) such that \(u \circ f = f\) and \(f \circ v = f\).

The uniqueness of the identity arrow proved above for a fixed object \(b\) rested on \(e\) and \(e'\) already sharing the same domain and codomain, which made \(e \circ e'\) automatically defined. Once objects are gone, that composability can no longer be assumed and has to be extracted from the Associativity axiom itself.

Lemma 1 (Uniqueness of units). Let \(f \in A\). If \(u\) and \(u'\) are units with \(u \circ f = f\) and \(u' \circ f = f\), then \(u = u'\). Likewise, if \(v\) and \(v'\) are units with \(f \circ v = f\) and \(f \circ v' = f\), then \(v = v'\).

Proof. We prove the first statement; the second is symmetric. Since \(u \circ f = f\), the expression \(u' \circ (u \circ f)\) equals \(u' \circ f\), which is defined by hypothesis. By Associativity, since \(u' \circ (u \circ f)\) is defined, so are \(u \circ f\) (already known) and \(u' \circ u\). As \(u'\) is a unit and \(u' \circ u\) is defined, \(u' \circ u = u\); as \(u\) is a unit and \(u' \circ u\) is defined, \(u' \circ u = u'\). Hence \(u = u' \circ u = u'\). ■

Definition 1 (Domain and codomain). By the Existence of Units axiom and Lemma 1, for each \(f \in A\) there is a unique unit \(u\) with \(u \circ f = f\) and a unique unit \(v\) with \(f \circ v = f\). Write \(\mathrm{cod}(f) := u\) and \(\mathrm{dom}(f) := v\).
Lemma 2. If \(u\) is a unit, then \(\mathrm{cod}(u) = \mathrm{dom}(u) = u\).

Proof. Since \(u\) is a unit, \(u \circ u\) is defined and \(u \circ u = u\). Thus \(u\) itself satisfies the defining property of \(\mathrm{cod}(u)\), so \(u = \mathrm{cod}(u)\) by Lemma 1; the same equality gives \(u = \mathrm{dom}(u)\). ■

Lemma 3 (Composability criterion). For \(f, g \in A\), \(g \circ f\) is defined if and only if \(\mathrm{cod}(f) = \mathrm{dom}(g)\).

Proof. (\(\Rightarrow\)) Suppose \(g \circ f\) is defined; write \(u := \mathrm{cod}(f)\), so \(u \circ f = f\). Then \(g \circ (u \circ f) = g \circ f\) is defined, so by Associativity \(u \circ f\) (known) and \(g \circ u\) are both defined. Since \(u\) is a unit and \(g \circ u\) is defined, \(g \circ u = g\); thus \(u\) is a unit satisfying \(g \circ u = g\), so \(u = \mathrm{dom}(g)\) by Lemma 1. Hence \(\mathrm{cod}(f) = \mathrm{dom}(g)\).

(\(\Leftarrow\)) Suppose \(\mathrm{cod}(f) = \mathrm{dom}(g) =: u\). Then \(u \circ f = f\) and \(g \circ u = g\) are both defined, so by Associativity \(g \circ (u \circ f)\) is defined. Since \(u \circ f = f\), this composite is \(g \circ f\); hence \(g \circ f\) is defined. ■

Theorem 1 (Equivalence of the two axiomatizations). The objects-and-arrows axioms for a metacategory given above (Associativity and Identity Law, together with objects, domain, and codomain) and the arrows-only axioms of Definition [Arrows-only] determine the same class of structures:
  1. If \(C\) is an objects-and-arrows metacategory, then \(\mathrm{Ar}(C)\), with its given partial composition, is an arrows-only metacategory whose units are exactly the identity arrows \(\{\mathrm{id}_b\}_{b \in \mathrm{Ob}(C)}\).
  2. If \((A, \circ)\) is an arrows-only metacategory, then \(\mathrm{Ob} := \{u \in A : u \text{ is a unit}\}\), \(\mathrm{Ar} := A\), \(\mathrm{dom}\) and \(\mathrm{cod}\) as in Definition 1, composition restricted to pairs with \(\mathrm{cod}(f) = \mathrm{dom}(g)\), and \(\mathrm{id}_u := u\), define an objects-and-arrows metacategory.
  3. These two constructions are mutually inverse.

Proof. (1) The Associativity and Identity Law axioms of \(C\), together with the fact that \(g \circ f\) is defined exactly when \(\mathrm{cod}(f) = \mathrm{dom}(g)\), give the Associativity axiom of Definition [Arrows-only] directly. For Existence of Units, given \(f : a \to b\) in \(C\), the arrows \(\mathrm{id}_b\) and \(\mathrm{id}_a\) satisfy \(\mathrm{id}_b \circ f = f\) and \(f \circ \mathrm{id}_a = f\) by the Identity Law, and each \(\mathrm{id}_c\) is a unit: \(\mathrm{id}_c \circ \mathrm{id}_c = \mathrm{id}_c\) by the Identity Law applied to \(f = \mathrm{id}_c\), and \(\mathrm{id}_c\) absorbs on both sides by the same law. These identity arrows are the only units. Suppose \(u\) is a unit of \((\mathrm{Ar}(C), \circ)\); write \(u : a \to a'\) in \(C\). Since \(u \circ u\) is defined, \(a = a'\), so \(u : a \to a\). Now \(\mathrm{id}_a \circ u\) is defined, so \(\mathrm{id}_a \circ u = u\) by the Identity Law; but since \(u\) is a unit and \(\mathrm{id}_a \circ u\) is defined, \(\mathrm{id}_a \circ u = \mathrm{id}_a\) as well. Hence \(u = \mathrm{id}_a\).

(2) Associativity for the constructed data is immediate from Definition [Arrows-only] together with Lemma 3. The Identity Law, for \(f\) with \(\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{cod}(f)} := \mathrm{cod}(f)\) and \(\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{dom}(f)} := \mathrm{dom}(f)\), reads \(\mathrm{cod}(f) \circ f = f\) and \(f \circ \mathrm{dom}(f) = f\), which is exactly Definition 1.

(3) Starting from \(C\), converting and converting back recovers the same objects (by the classification of units in (1)), the same \(\mathrm{dom}\) and \(\mathrm{cod}\) (since \(C\)'s own \(\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{dom}(f)}, \mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{cod}(f)}\) already satisfy Definition 1, hence coincide with the reconstructed ones by Lemma 1), and the same composition. Starting from \((A, \circ)\), converting and converting back recovers the same class \(A\), the same units (by (1)'s argument, a unit of the reconstructed metacategory is exactly \(\mathrm{id}_u = u\) for \(u\) an original unit), and the same \(\circ\), whose domain of definition is unchanged by Lemma 3. ■

¶ 2. Incidentally, this "arrows-only" reformulation exemplifies the structuralist ethos underlying category theory, in which objects are characterized entirely by their relational properties within the network of arrows, rather than by any intrinsic internal content. Within this established foundation, one takes a category to be any interpretation of the metacategory axioms within a standard (ZFC) set-theoretic background. To formally define a category, we follow the same steps used to define a metacategory in the beginning of this section:

Definition 2 (Direct Graph). A direct graph is a set \(O\) of objects, a set \(A\) of arrows and two functions \[ A \underset{\mathrm{cod}}{\overset{\mathrm{dom}}{\rightleftarrows}} O. \] The set of composable pairs of arrows is the set \[ A \times_O A = \{ \langle g,f \rangle \mid g,f \in A \; \text{and} \; \operatorname{dom}(g) = \operatorname{cod}(f) \}, \] denoted by product over O.

Thus:

Definition 3 (Category). A category is a graph with two additional functions, \[ \begin{aligned} O &\xrightarrow{\operatorname{id}} A, & A \times_O A &\xrightarrow{\circ} A, \\ c &\longmapsto \operatorname{id}_c, & \langle g, f \rangle &\longmapsto g \circ f, \end{aligned} \] called identity and composition, such that, for all \(a \in O\) and composable pairs of arrows \(\langle f,g \rangle \in A \times_O A\), the Associativity and Unit axioms and following properties holds: \(\operatorname{dom}(\operatorname{id}_a) = a = \operatorname{cod}(\operatorname{id}_a)\), \(\operatorname{dom}(g \circ f) = \operatorname{dom}(f)\) and \(\operatorname{cod}(g \circ f) = \operatorname{cod}(g)\).

Conventionally, the collection of objects of a category \( \mathscr{C} \) is denoted by \(\operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\), and the collection of its arrows (henceforth, morphisms) by \(\operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\). For any two objects \(a,b \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\), the collection of morphisms \(a \rightarrow b\), called a hom-set, is denoted by \(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{C}}(a,b) := \{ f \in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C}) \mid \operatorname{dom}(f) = a \text{ and } \operatorname{cod}(f) = b \}\).

Remark 1 (Small and large categories). A category \(\mathscr{C}\) is small if \(\operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\) and \(\operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\) are both sets, and large otherwise; it is locally small if \(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{C}}(a,b)\) is a set for every \(a,b\), irrespective of the size of \(\operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\) itself. Mac Lane (the main resource for this section) resolves the resulting set-theoretic difficulties, e.g. \(\mathbf{Set}\) is locally small although its objects do not form a set, by working relative to a fixed universe.

¶ 2. Furthermore, a small category can be elegantly defined as a monoid object in the category of directed graphs (or spans) over a fixed vertex set \(O = \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\).

Proposition 1 (Categories as internal monoids). Let \(\mathscr{C}\) be a small category (Remark 1), and set \(O := \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\), \(M := \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\), equipped with \(s := \operatorname{dom}\), \(t := \operatorname{cod} : M \to O\). Form the fibered product (or the pullback) of \(M\) with itself over \(O\), \[ M \times_O M := \{ (g,f) \in M \times M \mid s(g) = t(f) \}, \] the set of composable pairs, and, iterating, the set \(M \times_O M \times_O M\) of composable triples. Definition 3 then states precisely that:
  • (Composition) there is a map \(\circ : M \times_O M \to M\);
  • (Associativity) the two induced maps \(M \times_O M \times_O M \to M\), given by \((h,g,f) \mapsto (h \circ g) \circ f\) and \((h,g,f) \mapsto h \circ (g \circ f)\), coincide;
  • (Identity) there is a map \(\operatorname{id} : O \to M\) such that \(\operatorname{id}_b \circ f = f = f \circ \operatorname{id}_a\) whenever \(s(f)=a\), \(t(f)=b\).
Equivalently: \(M\), together with \(s,t : M \to O\), is a monoid object in the category \(\mathbf{Graph}(O)\) of graphs with fixed vertex set \(O\) (equivalently, endo-spans \(O \xleftarrow{s} M \xrightarrow{t} O\) in \(\mathbf{Set}\)), with respect to the monoidal product \(\times_O\) and unit the identity span \(O \xleftarrow{\operatorname{id}} O \xrightarrow{\operatorname{id}} O\). When \(O\) is a singleton, \(\times_O\) reduces to the ordinary product \(\times\), and one recovers exactly Example 2; in other words, a small category is, precisely, the many-object generalization of a monoid.
Remark 2 (The duality principle). To every category \(\mathscr{C}\) one associates its opposite \(\mathscr{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\), with \(\operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C}^{\mathrm{op}}) := \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\) and \(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{C}^{\mathrm{op}}}(a,b) := \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{C}}(b,a)\), composition being inherited from \(\mathscr{C}\) in reverse order. Since the axioms of Definition 3 are symmetric under this reversal, Mac Lane's duality principle asserts that every valid statement of category theory, expressed solely via objects, morphisms, composition, and identities, remains valid upon systematically reversing all arrows, i.e. upon replacing \(\mathscr{C}\) by \(\mathscr{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\) throughout.
Example 1 (Discrete Category). A category \(\mathscr{D}\) is said to be discrete if its only morphisms are the identity morphisms. Formally, for every \(f \in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{D})\), there exists an object \(a \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{D})\) such that \(f = \operatorname{id}_a\).
Example 2 (Monoid). A monoid can be equivalently defined as a category with a single object, say \(\star\). Consequently, any two morphisms in \(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{C}}(\star, \star)\) can be composed. Thus, a monoid is characterized as a set \(M = \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\) equipped with a binary operation \(M \times M \rightarrow M\) (representing morphism composition) which is associative and admits an identity element. In other words, a monoid is a semigroup with an identity element.
Example 3 (Groupoid). A category \(\mathscr{G}\) is a groupoid if every morphism is invertible: for each \(f : a \to b\) there exists \(f^{-1} : b \to a\) with \(f^{-1} \circ f = \operatorname{id}_a\) and \(f \circ f^{-1} = \operatorname{id}_b\). A discrete category (Example 1) is trivially a groupoid, each identity being its own inverse; a one-object groupoid is precisely a group, exactly as a one-object category is a monoid (Example 2). A groupoid is thus the common refinement of Examples 1 and 2, obtained by additionally requiring invertibility.

¶ 3. Beyond these structural and somewhat fundamental examples, category theory furnishes the natural language for several fields in physics and mathematics, such as constructions central to both quantum mechanics formalism and quantization, both geometric and algebraic.

Example 4 (Category of Hilbert Spaces). In the categorical formulation of quantum mechanics, one studies the category \(\mathbf{Hilb}\), whose objects are complex Hilbert spaces and whose morphisms are bounded linear operators. It carries a symmetric monoidal structure given by the (completed) Hilbert-space tensor product \(\otimes\), essential for describing composite quantum systems and entanglement. Moreover, the assignment \(f \mapsto f^\dagger\) of the adjoint operator defines, in the language of Remark 2, an identity-on-objects functor \((-)^\dagger : \mathbf{Hilb}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathbf{Hilb}\) with \((-)^{\dagger\dagger} = \operatorname{id}\); this involutive structure, standard in categorical quantum mechanics, singles out the physically distinguished self-adjoint morphisms (observables) and unitary isomorphisms (symmetries).
Example 5 (Symplectic Category and Lagrangian Correspondences). Weinstein's symplectic category \(\mathbf{Symp}\) has as objects symplectic manifolds \((M,\omega)\). A morphism from \((M_1,\omega_1)\) to \((M_2,\omega_2)\) is a canonical relation: a Lagrangian submanifold \(L \subset \overline{M_1} \times M_2\), where \(\overline{M_1} := (M_1,-\omega_1)\). Given \(L_1 \subset \overline{M_1} \times M_2\) and \(L_2 \subset \overline{M_2} \times M_3\), their composite is defined, as for ordinary relations, by \[ L_2 \circ L_1 := \{ (x,z) \mid \exists\, y \in M_2,\ (x,y)\in L_1,\ (y,z)\in L_2 \}, \] which is Lagrangian provided the defining fibered product is transverse (or clean); absent this hypothesis \(L_2 \circ L_1\) may fail even to be a smooth submanifold, so that \(\mathbf{Symp}\) satisfies Definition 3 only generically — a defect repaired by Wehrheim and Woodward through the formal adjunction of composites. The symplectic category nonetheless furnishes the intended domain of a (still largely conjectural) quantization functor \(Q : \mathbf{Symp} \to \mathbf{Hilb}\), assigning to each classical phase space a Hilbert space of quantum states.
Example 6 (Category of C*-Algebras and Gelfand--Naimark Duality). Let \(\mathbf{C^*Alg}\) denote the category of unital C*-algebras and unital *-homomorphisms. Its full subcategory \(\mathbf{CommC^*Alg}\) of commutative unital C*-algebras is, by the Gelfand--Naimark theorem, dually equivalent to the category \(\mathbf{CompHaus}\) of compact Hausdorff spaces: the functor \(C(-) : \mathbf{CompHaus}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathbf{CommC^*Alg}\), sending \(X\) to its algebra of continuous complex-valued functions, is an equivalence. This is a concrete instance of the duality principle (Remark 2), in which \(\mathbf{CompHaus}^{\mathrm{op}}\) is equivalent to a category of an entirely different, algebraic nature, and it renders precise the passage, central to quantization, from a commutative algebra of classical observables to a possibly noncommutative one. Every Hilbert space \(H\) yields the C*-algebra \(B(H)\) of bounded operators; although \(H \mapsto B(H)\) is not functorial on all of \(\mathbf{Hilb}\), it restricts to a functor from the wide subgroupoid of \(\mathbf{Hilb}\) whose morphisms are unitary isomorphisms to the subgroupoid of \(\mathbf{C^*Alg}\) spanned by *-isomorphisms, via \(u \mapsto (T \mapsto uTu^{-1})\), thereby relating this example to Example 4.
Example 7 (Cobordism Category and TQFTs). Fundamental to the axiomatization of Topological Quantum Field Theories (TQFTs) is the cobordism category, denoted by \(\mathbf{Cob}_n\). The objects are closed, oriented \((n-1)\)-dimensional manifolds, and the morphisms are equivalence classes of \(n\)-dimensional oriented cobordisms between them. Composition of morphisms corresponds to the topological gluing of manifolds along their boundaries.

¶ 4. Until now, we have worked with the notion of maps between objects within a category, namely, the aforementioned morphisms (or, more abstractly, arrows, in the case of metacategories). There is, however, a broader notion: that of morphisms between categories themselves. Such morphisms are called functors. Formally, we define:

Definition 4 (Functor). Let \(\mathscr{C}\) and \(\mathscr{D}\) be categories. A functor \(\mathrm{F}: \mathscr{C} \rightarrow \mathscr{D}\) with \(\operatorname{dom}(\mathrm{F}) = \mathscr{C}\) and \(\operatorname{cod}(\mathrm{F}) = \mathscr{D}\) consists of a pair of functions \((\mathrm{F}_\mathrm{ob}, \mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{ar}})\), where \(\mathrm{F}_\mathrm{ob}\) acts on elements of \(\operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\) as \(T(a) = Ta \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{D})\), and \(\mathrm{F}_\mathrm{ar}\) acts on elements of \(\operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\) as \((a \xrightarrow{}[f] a') \mapsto (\mathrm{F}f:\mathrm{F}a \rightarrow \mathrm{F}c')\) in such a way that \[ \mathrm{F} \operatorname{id}_\mathscr{C} = \operatorname{id}_{T_\mathscr{C}} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathrm{F} (g \circ f) = \mathrm{F}g \circ \mathrm{F}f \] for any composable pair \(\langle f, g \rangle \in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\).
Remark 3 (Notation). The use of the notation \(\mathrm{F}_\mathrm{ob}\) and \(\mathrm{F}_\mathrm{ar}\) were due to pedagogical purposes. But, henceforth, and to avoid cumbersome notation, we will refer to both as \(\mathrm{F}\).

Analogously, a functor can be described in an "arrow only" framework, where \(\mathrm{F}\) is treated as a function that maps arrows \(f \in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\) to arrows \(\mathrm{F}f \in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{D})\), mapping identity onto identity and, for each composable pair \(f \circ g \in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\), one has a associated composable pair \(\mathrm{F}f \circ \mathrm{F}g \in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{D})\).

Example 8 (Quantization as a functor). The symplectic category \(\mathbf{Symp}\) has symplectic manifolds as objects and, in the construction described above, suitably composable Lagrangian correspondences as morphisms. A quantization procedure may be organized as a functor \(Q:\mathbf{Symp}\longrightarrow\mathbf{Hilb}\). At the level of objects, \(Q\) assigns to a classical phase space \((M,\omega)\) a Hilbert space \(Q(M,\omega)\) of quantum states. At the level of morphisms, a Lagrangian correspondence \(L:M_1\longrightarrow M_2\) is assigned an operator \(Q(L):Q(M_1,\omega_1)\longrightarrow Q(M_2,\omega_2)\). An essential requirement is that they respect the categorical structure: \[ Q(1_M)=1_{Q(M)}, \qquad Q(L_2\circ L_1)=Q(L_2)\circ Q(L_1) \] whenever the composite \(L_2\circ L_1\) is defined. Thus the functor turns composition of classical processes into composition of quantum operators. It is worth emphasizing that the notation \(Q:\mathbf{Symp}\to\mathbf{Hilb}\) denotes a particular quantization functor only after a quantization construction satisfying these properties has been specified. The categorical point is purely abstract, and it aims only to structurally organize and establish the construction of such functor.
Example 9 (Topological quantum field theory). Consider the cobordism category \(\mathbf{Cob}_n\), whose objects are closed oriented \((n-1)\)-dimensional manifolds and whose morphisms are \(n\)-dimensional oriented cobordisms, up to the chosen notion of equivalence. An \(n\)-dimensional topological quantum field theory is organized as a symmetric monoidal functor \(Z:\mathbf{Cob}_n\longrightarrow\mathbf{Hilb}\). At the object level, \(\Sigma\longmapsto Z(\Sigma)\) assigns a Hilbert space to each spatial manifold. At the morphism level, a cobordism \(M:\Sigma_0\longrightarrow\Sigma_1\) is assigned an operator \(Z(M):Z(\Sigma_0)\longrightarrow Z(\Sigma_1)\). Gluing cobordisms corresponds to composition of operators: \[ Z(M_2\circ M_1) = Z(M_2)\circ Z(M_1), \] and the identity cylinder on \(\Sigma\) is sent to the identity operator on \(Z(\Sigma)\). The monoidal structure adds further compatibility. Disjoint union of manifolds is sent to the tensor product of Hilbert spaces, \[ Z(\Sigma_1\sqcup\Sigma_2) \cong Z(\Sigma_1)\otimes Z(\Sigma_2), \] and disjoint union of cobordisms is sent to tensor product of the corresponding operators.

¶ 5. There are some special kinds of functors worth mentioning before we venture forth:

  • a functor \(\mathrm{F}: \mathscr{C} \rightarrow \mathscr{D}\) is forgetful when it forgets some algebraic structure(s) of objects of \(\mathscr{C}\). For example, the functor \(U: \mathrm{Grp} \rightarrow \operatorname{Set}\) assigns to each group \(G\) the set \(UG\) of its elements, forgetting the multiplication --- and, hence, the group structure --- defined on \(G\).
  • a functor \(\mathrm{F}: \mathscr{C} \rightarrow \mathscr{D}\) is full when, for each pair \(c, c' \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\) and for every arrow \(\mathrm{F}c \xrightarrow{g} \mathrm{F}c'\), there is a arrow \(c \xrightarrow{f} c' \in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\) with \(g = \operatorname{F}f\).
  • a functor \(\mathrm{F}: \mathscr{C} \rightarrow \mathscr{D}\) is faithful when, to every pair \(c, c' \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\) and to every pair \(c \xrightarrow{f_1, f_2} c'\in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\), the equality \(\operatorname{F}c \xrightarrow{\mathrm{F}f_1 = \mathrm{F}f_2} \operatorname{F}c'\) implies \(f_1 = f_2\). An example of functor that is both full and faithful, is the functor \(2\text{-}\mathrm{Vects} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{F}} \mathrm{Ch}_{[-1,0]}(\mathrm{Vects})\). In particular, \(\mathrm{F}\) is also an almost surjective functor, and establishes the equivalence that \(\mathrm{F}\) is an equivalence of categories \(\Leftrightarrow\) \(V^\bullet \xrightarrow{f} W^\bullet\) is a quasi-isomorphism, a particular case of the Dold-Kan theorem.

  • \(\mathscr{F}(U \hookrightarrow U) = \operatorname{id}_{\mathscr{F}U}\)

¶ 7. One can use natural transformations --- or, more precisely, natural isomorphisms --- to formulate the notion of equivalence of categories. This can be understood as a categorical analogue of homotopy equivalence: functors need not be strict inverses, but only inverses up to natural isomorphism. In this perspective, the terminal category \(\mathbb{I}\), consisting of a single object and its identity morphism, plays the role of a point, while natural transformations provide a categorical analogue of homotopies [Riehl, 2016].

Definition 6 (Equivalence of Categories). Consider functors \(\mathscr{C}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{F}}\mathscr{D}\) and \(\mathscr{D}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{G}}\mathscr{C}\). An equivalence of categories consists of \(\mathrm{F}\) and \(\mathrm{G}\), together with natural isomorphisms \[ \eta:\operatorname{id}_{\mathscr{C}}\cong \mathrm{G}\circ\mathrm{F}, \qquad \tau:\mathrm{F}\circ\mathrm{G}\cong\operatorname{id}_{\mathscr{D}}. \] Categories \(\mathscr{C}\) and \(\mathscr{D}\) are equivalent, written \(\mathscr{C} \cong \mathscr{D}\), if there exists an equivalence between them. A functor \(\mathrm{F}:\mathscr{C}\rightarrow\mathscr{D}\) is an equivalence if it is part of such an equivalence of categories.
Example 12 (Point and Partial Functions Sets). The functors \(\mathbf{Set}_\partial \xrightarrow{(-)} \mathbf{Set}_{*}\) and \(\mathbf{Set}_{*} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{U}} \mathbf{Set}_\partial\), with \(U\circ (-)_+ \cong \operatorname{id}_{\mathbf{Set}_\partial}\) and \((-)_+ \circ U \cong \operatorname{id}_{\mathbf{Set}_{*}}\), define an equivalence of categories between the category of pointed sets and the category of sets and partial functions.

¶ 9. One of the foundational concepts of category theory is that of duality. As established in Remark 2, this concept --- formally and axiomatically developed by Mac Lane [Mac Lane, 1998] --- naturally yields the notion of the opposite category, \(\mathscr{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\). Having delineated how duality reverses arrows and composition within a single category, the natural subsequent inquiry concerns its effect on functors. Consider a functor \(\mathrm{F}: \mathscr{C} \rightarrow \mathscr{D}\), which acts as \(c \mapsto \mathrm{F}c\) on each \(c \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\) and as \(f \mapsto \mathrm{F}f\) on each \(f \in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\). A functor \(\mathrm{F}^\mathrm{op}: \mathscr{C}^\mathrm{op} \rightarrow \mathscr{D}^\mathrm{op}\) is defined as preserving the action on objects of \(\mathscr{C}\) and, as expected, acting on morphisms of \(\mathscr{C}^\mathrm{op}\) as \(f^\mathrm{op} \mapsto (\mathrm{F}f)^\mathrm{op}\). From this standpoint, one can characterize two kinds of functors:

  • Consider a functor \(\mathrm{G}: \mathscr{C}^\mathrm{op} \rightarrow \mathscr{D}\) that assigns to each \(c \in \operatorname{Ob}( \mathscr{C}^\mathrm{op})\) a corresponding \(\mathrm{G}c \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{D})\), and to each arrow \(b \xrightarrow{f^\mathrm{op}} a \in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C}^\mathrm{op})\) a corresponding arrow \(\mathrm{G}b \xrightarrow{\mathrm{G}f^\mathrm{op}} \mathrm{G}a \in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{D})\), with composite defined as \(\mathrm{G}(f^\mathrm{op} \circ g^\mathrm{op}) = \mathrm{G} f^\mathrm{op} \circ \mathrm{G} g^\mathrm{op}\), whenever \(f^\mathrm{op} \circ g^\mathrm{op}\) is defined. By describing \(\mathrm{G}\) in terms of \(\mathscr{C}\), one writes \(\overline{\mathrm{G}} f\) for \(\mathrm{G} f^\mathrm{op}\) and (implicitly) defines a "new" functor \[ \mathscr{C} \xrightarrow{\overline{\mathrm{G}}} \mathscr{D}, \] which maps arrows \(a \xrightarrow{f} b\) to arrows \(\overline{\mathrm{G}}b \xrightarrow{\overline{\mathrm{G}}f} \overline{\mathrm{G}}a\). Such a functor is called a contravariant functor;
  • A functor \(\mathrm{F}: \mathscr{C} \rightarrow \mathscr{D}\) as we originally defined it --- that is, one that does not invert arrows --- is called a covariant functor.

Example (Bases of Vector Spaces). Let \(\mathbf{Vct}_K\) denote the category in which \[\operatorname{Ob}(\mathbf{Vct}_K) = \{V \mid V \; \text{is a vector field over the fixed field } \; K\} \] and \(\operatorname{Mor}(\mathbf{Vct}_K) = \{\mathrm{T} \mid T: V \rightarrow W \; \text{is a linear transformation}, \; \text{for all} \;W, V \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathbf{Vct}_K)\}\), and consider the forgetful functor \(\mathbf{Vct}_K \xrightarrow{\mathrm{U}} \mathbf{Set}\), that maps a vector space \(V\) to the set of its elements. For any set \(X \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathbf{Set})\) there is an associated vector space \(V_X\) that consists of all formal \(K\)-linear combinations of the elements of \(X\) --- that is, \(X\) forms a basis for \(V_X\). There is a \(X \xrightarrow{j} U(V_X)\) such that, for any other vector space \(W \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathbf{Vec}_K)\), any \(V_X \xrightarrow{f} U(W)\) can be extended to a unique linear transformation \(V_X \xrightarrow{f'} W\) with \(Uf' \circ j = f\), meaning that \(j\) is a universal arrow from \(X\) to \(U\).
Example (Complete Metric Spaces). Let \(\mathbf{Met}\) be the category whose objects are metric spaces, that is, \(\operatorname{Ob}(\mathbf{Met})=\{(X,d_X)\mid X\text{ is a set and }d_X:X\times X\xrightarrow{} \mathbb{R}_{\geq0}\text{ is a metric on }X\}\), and whose morphisms are non-expansive maps, \(\operatorname{Mor}_{(X,Y)}(\mathbf{Met})=\{f\mid f:X\xrightarrow{}Y,\ d_Y(f(x),f(x'))\leq d_X(x,x')\text{ for all }x,x'\in X\}\). The complete metric spaces, that is, those for which every Cauchy sequence \((x_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq X\) converges to some \(x\in X\), form a full subcategory \(\mathbf{CMet}\subseteq\mathbf{Met}\). Let \(\mathbf{CMet}\xrightarrow{U} \mathbf{Met}\) be the inclusion functor. For every \(X\in\mathbf{Met}\), its completion consists of a complete metric space \(\overline{X}\) together with an isometric embedding \(X\xrightarrow{\eta_X}U(\overline{X})\) whose image is dense in \(U(\overline{X})\). Moreover, for every \(Y\in\mathbf{CMet}\) and every non-expansive map \(X\xrightarrow{f}U(Y)\), there exists a unique non-expansive map \(\overline{X}\xrightarrow{\overline{f}}Y\) such that \(f=U(\overline{f})\circ\eta_X\). Thus, \(X\xrightarrow{\eta_X}U(\overline{X})\) is a universal arrow from \(X\) to \(U\).

As should be expected at this point in the text, this concept, analogously to the natural transformation, admits a formulation in terms of hom-sets:

Proposition (Universal Arrow and Hom-sets). For a functor \(\mathscr{D} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{G}} \mathscr{C}\) a pair \(\langle r, c \xrightarrow{u} \mathrm{G}r \rangle\) is universal from \(c\) to \(\mathrm{G}\) \(\Leftrightarrow\) each mapping \((r \xrightarrow{f'} d) \mapsto (c \xrightarrow{\mathrm{G}f' \circ u} \mathrm{G}d)\) is a bijection of hom-sets, \(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{D}}(r,d) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{C}} (c, \mathrm{G}d)\). Such bijection is natural in \(d \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{D})\). Conversely, given \(r\) and \(c\), any natural isomorphism \(\eta: \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{D}}(r,d) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{C}} (c, \mathrm{G}d)\) is determined by a unique arrow \(c \xrightarrow{u} \mathrm{G}r\) such that \(\langle r, u \rangle\) is universal from \(c\) to \(\mathrm{G}\).

Proof. The statement that \(\langle r, u \rangle\) is universal is precisely the condition that the mapping \(f' \mapsto \mathrm{G}f' \circ u\) is a bijection. This bijection is natural in \(d\); indeed, for any \(g: d \to d'\), one has \(\mathrm{G}(g \circ f') \circ u = \mathrm{G}g \circ (\mathrm{G}f' \circ u)\), which verifies the naturality requirement.

Definition (Representable Functors). Let the category \(\mathscr{D}\) have small hom-sets, that is, a locally small category. A representation of a functor \(\mathscr{D}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{F}} \mathbf{Set}\) is a pair \(\langle r, \psi \rangle\), where \(r \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{D})\) and \[ \psi: \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{D}}(r, -) \cong \mathrm{F}\] is a natural isomorphism. The object \(r\) is called the representing object and the functor \(\mathrm{F}\) is said to be representable when such a representation exists.

In other words, a representable functor is, up to isomorphism, a covariant hom-functor \(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{D}}(r,-)\). One can relate such notion to that of universal arrows:

Proposition (Representable Functors and Universal Arrows). Let \( \{\star\}\) denote the one-point set and let \(\mathscr{D}\) be locally small. Given the functor \(\mathscr{D} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{F}} \mathbf{Set}\), if \(\langle r, \{\star\} \xrightarrow{u_\star} \mathrm{F}r \rangle\) is a universal arrow, then the function \((r \xrightarrow{f'} d) \xrightarrow{\psi} \mathrm{F}f' \circ u_\star \in \mathrm{F}d\) is a representation of \(\mathrm{F}\), for each \(d \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{D})\). Every representation of \(\mathrm{F}\) is obtained in this way, from exactly one such universal arrow.

Proof. For any set \(X \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathbf{Set})\), a function \(\{\star\} \xrightarrow{f} X\) is completely determined by \(f(\star)\in X\). The correspondence \(f \mapsto f(\star)\) is a bijection \(\mathbf{Set}(\star, X) \rightarrow X\), natural in \(X\in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathbf{Set})\). Its composition with \(\mathrm{F}\) yields a natural isomorphism \(\mathbf{Set}(\star, \mathrm{F}-) \xrightarrow{\cong} \mathrm{F}\). This result, in consonance with the representation \(\psi\) of Definition (Representable Functors) yields \[ \mathbf{Set}(\star, \mathrm{F}-) \cong \mathrm{F} \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{D}}(r,-).\] Therefore, a representation of \(\mathrm{F}\) amounts to a natural isomorphism \( \mathbf{Set}(\star, \mathrm{F}-) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{D}}(r,-)\). So, the desired result follows from Proposition (Universal Arrow and Hom-sets). ■

In retrospect, the takeaway is that a universal arrow \(c \xrightarrow{u} \mathrm{F}r\) (for a functor \(\mathrm{F}: \mathscr{D} \to \mathscr{C}\)) is simply an isomorphism of hom-sets \(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{D}}(r,d) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{C}}(c, \mathrm{F}d)\) (Proposition Universal Arrow and Hom-sets) and, therefore, it defines a representation of the functor \(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{C}}(c, \mathrm{F}-): \mathscr{D} \to \mathbf{Set}\). Equivalently, Proposition (Representable Functors and Universal Arrows) shows that representations can be understood concretely as universal arrows from a one-point set; while Definition (Representable Functors) makes it clear that a representation itself is fundamentally just a natural isomorphism of functors. Finally, generalizing this idea from universal elements to arbitrary elements allows us to properly introduce the famous Yoneda Lemma:

There is a addendum to the Yoneda Lemma that takes a form of a proposition in [Mac Lane, 1998], but to formally introduce it one need the notion of product of categories:

Definition (Product of Categories). From two given categories \(\mathscr{B}\) and \(\mathscr{C}\), we construct a new category \(\mathscr{B} \times \mathscr{C}\), called the product of \(\mathscr{B}\) and \(\mathscr{C}\), as follows. An object of \(\mathscr{B} \times \mathscr{C}\) is a pair \(\langle b, c \rangle\), where \(b \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{B})\) and \(c \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\). A morphism \(\langle b, c \rangle \xrightarrow{\langle f, g \rangle} \langle b', c' \rangle\) of \(\mathscr{B} \times \mathscr{C}\) is a pair \(\langle f, g \rangle\) consisting of morphisms \(b \xrightarrow{f} b'\) in \(\mathscr{B}\) and \(c \xrightarrow{g} c'\) in \(\mathscr{C}\). The composite of two such morphisms \[ \langle b, c \rangle \xrightarrow{\langle f, g \rangle} \langle b', c' \rangle \xrightarrow{\langle f', g' \rangle} \langle b'', c'' \rangle \] is defined in terms of the composites in \(\mathscr{B}\) and \(\mathscr{C}\) by \[ \langle f', g' \rangle \circ \langle f, g \rangle = \langle f' \circ f, g' \circ g \rangle. \] Functors \(\mathscr{B} \times \mathscr{C} \xrightarrow{P} \mathscr{B}\) and \(\mathscr{B} \times \mathscr{C} \xrightarrow{Q} \mathscr{C}\), called the projections of the product, are defined on objects and morphisms by \(P\langle f, g \rangle = f\) and \(Q\langle f, g \rangle = g\). They have the following property: given any category \(\mathscr{D}\) and two functors \(\mathscr{D} \xrightarrow{R} \mathscr{B}\) and \(\mathscr{D} \xrightarrow{T} \mathscr{C}\), there is a unique functor \(\mathscr{D} \xrightarrow{F} \mathscr{B} \times \mathscr{C}\) such that \(P \circ F = R\) and \(Q \circ F = T\). Explicitly, these two conditions require that \(Fh\), for any morphism \(h\) in \(\mathscr{D}\), must be \(\langle Rh, Th \rangle\).
