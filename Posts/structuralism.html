§ I. Mathesis as Structure: Categories, HoTT, and Subjectless Structuralism

§ I.A Formal Framework I: Category Theory and the Yoneda Lemma

¶ 1. The concept of a category can be formalized within two primary theoretical frameworks. The first relies on a purely axiomatic approach, wherein these structures are designated as metacategories. The second framework, which serves a natural extetion — or, more rigorously, a particular case — of the former, represents the standard formulation of categories within Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory with Axiom of Choice (ZFC). We thus commence our exposition by introducing this strictly axiomatic characterization:

Definition 1 (Metagraph). A Metagraph comprises a collection of objects \(a,b,c,\dots\), a collection of arrows \(f,g,h\dots\) and two operations, defined as follows: (i) Domain, which assigns to each arrow \(f\) an object \(a = \operatorname{dom}(f)\); (ii) Codomain, which assigns to each arrow \(f\) an object \(b = \operatorname{cod}(f)\).

Diagrammatically, these operations may be depicted as arrows from \(a\) to \(b\), denoted by \(a \xrightarrow[]{f} b\). Consequently, a finite graph can be conceptualized as a concatenation of such arrows, expressed, for example, as \(a \xrightarrow[]{f} b \xrightarrow[]{g} c \xrightarrow[]{h}\). Equipped with the underlying structure of a metagraph, we can proceed to the definition of a metacategory:

Definition 2 (Metacategory). A \textit{metacategory} is a metagraph equipped with two additional operations, namely: (i) Identity, wich assigns to each object \(a\) an arrow \(a \xrightarrow[]{\mathrm{id}_a} a\); (ii) \textit{Composition}, commonly denoted by \(\circ\) or by \(\cdot\), which assigns to each pair \(\langle g, f \rangle\) of arrows with \(\operatorname{dom} (g) = \operatorname{cod} (f)\) an arrow \(\operatorname{dom} (f) \xrightarrow[]{g \circ f} \operatorname{cod} (g) \), which is called their composite, which is visually represented by the following commutative diagram:
Example 1. The metacategory of sets consists of all sets \(X, Y, \dots\) as objects and all functions between sets, \(f: X \rightarrow Y\), as arrows, equipped with standard function composition.
Example 2. The metacategory of groups consists of all groups \(G, H, \dots\) as objects and all group homomorphisms, \(f: G \rightarrow H\), as arrows, equipped with standard function composition.

Moreover, composition and the assignment of identities to objects are subject to the following axioms, understood to hold whenever the arrows in question are composable:

  • (Associativity): For any composable arrows \(a \xrightarrow{f} b \xrightarrow{g} c \xrightarrow{h} d\), one always has \(h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f\).
  • (Identity Law): For any arrows \(a \xrightarrow{f} b\) and \(b \xrightarrow{g} c\), the identity arrow \(\mathrm{id}_b\) satisfies \(\mathrm{id}_b \circ f = f\) and \(g \circ \mathrm{id}_b = g\).

Formally, a commutative diagram is defined as follows: let \(\Gamma\) be a diagram representing a collection of objects and arrows within a metacategory \(C\). We say that \(\Gamma\) commutes if, for any two objects \(x\) and \(y\) in \(\Gamma\), all directed paths of arrows connecting \(x\) to \(y\) yield the same composite arrow in \(C\). The two diagrams above are instances of this notion: in each, every directed path between a given pair of objects composes to the same arrow.

Furthermore, the identity arrow of an object \(b\) within a metacategory \(C\) is uniquely determined: if \(e\) and \(e'\) both satisfy the identity law for \(b\), then \(e = e \circ e' = e'\), the first equality using \(e\) as a left identity and the second using \(e'\) as a right identity. Consequently, it is common practice to identify this arrow directly with the object itself, establishing the notational equivalence \(b = \mathrm{id}_b\). This identification is licensed by the fact that identity arrows are themselves characterizable in purely arrow-theoretic terms --- as those arrows \(e\) satisfying \(e \circ f = f\) and \(g \circ e = g\) whenever the respective compositions are defined --- and stand in canonical bijective correspondence with the objects of the category. It thereby becomes possible to dispense with objects altogether, defining a category instead as a class of arrows equipped with a partial composition operation subject to the usual associativity and identity axioms, with objects recovered only derivatively, as the identity arrows themselves. The data for an arrows-only metacategory consists of a class of arrows \(A\), together with an operation that assigns to each composable pair \(\langle g, f \rangle\) their composite, namely, the arrow \(g \circ f\). In this context, one defines a unit (which will assume the role of the identity arrow) in \(A\) as follows:

Definition 3 (Unit). Let \(A\) be a class equipped with a partial binary operation of composition. An arrow \(u \in A\) is a unit in \(A\) if
  • \(u \circ u\) is defined and \(u \circ u = u\);
  • for every \(k \in A\), if \(u \circ k\) is defined then \(u \circ k = k\);
  • for every \(k \in A\), if \(k \circ u\) is defined then \(k \circ u = k\).

An arrow \(u\) that already sits inside an objects-and-arrows metacategory as some \(\mathrm{id}_b\) is trivially its own left and right unit. So, to dispense with objects, this absorbing behavior is taken as the definition of the arrows that will play their role. With this established, one can formally define an arrows-only metacategory as follows:

Definition 4 (Arrows-only Metacategory). An arrows-only metacategory is a class \(A\) equipped with a partial binary operation of composition, subject to the following axioms:
  • (Associativity): For all \(f, g, h \in A\), \(h \circ (g \circ f)\) is defined if and only if \(g \circ f\) and \(h \circ g\) are both defined; and in that case, \((h \circ g) \circ f\) is also defined and \(h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f\).
  • (Existence of Units): For every \(f \in A\), there exist units \(u, v\) (Definition 3) such that \(u \circ f = f\) and \(f \circ v = f\).

The uniqueness of the identity arrow proved above for a fixed object \(b\) rested on \(e\) and \(e'\) already sharing the same domain and codomain, which made \(e \circ e'\) automatically defined. Once objects are gone, that composability can no longer be assumed and has to be extracted from the Associativity axiom itself.

Lemma 1 (Uniqueness of units). Let \(f \in A\). If \(u\) and \(u'\) are units with \(u \circ f = f\) and \(u' \circ f = f\), then \(u = u'\). Likewise, if \(v\) and \(v'\) are units with \(f \circ v = f\) and \(f \circ v' = f\), then \(v = v'\).

Proof. We prove the first statement; the second is symmetric. Since \(u \circ f = f\), the expression \(u' \circ (u \circ f)\) equals \(u' \circ f\), which is defined by hypothesis. By Associativity, since \(u' \circ (u \circ f)\) is defined, so are \(u \circ f\) (already known) and \(u' \circ u\). As \(u'\) is a unit and \(u' \circ u\) is defined, \(u' \circ u = u\); as \(u\) is a unit and \(u' \circ u\) is defined, \(u' \circ u = u'\). Hence \(u = u' \circ u = u'\). ■

Definition 1 (Domain and codomain). By the Existence of Units axiom and Lemma 1, for each \(f \in A\) there is a unique unit \(u\) with \(u \circ f = f\) and a unique unit \(v\) with \(f \circ v = f\). Write \(\mathrm{cod}(f) := u\) and \(\mathrm{dom}(f) := v\).
Lemma 2. If \(u\) is a unit, then \(\mathrm{cod}(u) = \mathrm{dom}(u) = u\).

Proof. Since \(u\) is a unit, \(u \circ u\) is defined and \(u \circ u = u\). Thus \(u\) itself satisfies the defining property of \(\mathrm{cod}(u)\), so \(u = \mathrm{cod}(u)\) by Lemma 1; the same equality gives \(u = \mathrm{dom}(u)\). ■

Lemma 3 (Composability criterion). For \(f, g \in A\), \(g \circ f\) is defined if and only if \(\mathrm{cod}(f) = \mathrm{dom}(g)\).

Proof. (\(\Rightarrow\)) Suppose \(g \circ f\) is defined; write \(u := \mathrm{cod}(f)\), so \(u \circ f = f\). Then \(g \circ (u \circ f) = g \circ f\) is defined, so by Associativity \(u \circ f\) (known) and \(g \circ u\) are both defined. Since \(u\) is a unit and \(g \circ u\) is defined, \(g \circ u = g\); thus \(u\) is a unit satisfying \(g \circ u = g\), so \(u = \mathrm{dom}(g)\) by Lemma 1. Hence \(\mathrm{cod}(f) = \mathrm{dom}(g)\).

(\(\Leftarrow\)) Suppose \(\mathrm{cod}(f) = \mathrm{dom}(g) =: u\). Then \(u \circ f = f\) and \(g \circ u = g\) are both defined, so by Associativity \(g \circ (u \circ f)\) is defined. Since \(u \circ f = f\), this composite is \(g \circ f\); hence \(g \circ f\) is defined. ■

Theorem 1 (Equivalence of the two axiomatizations). The objects-and-arrows axioms for a metacategory given above (Associativity and Identity Law, together with objects, domain, and codomain) and the arrows-only axioms of Definition [Arrows-only] determine the same class of structures:
  1. If \(C\) is an objects-and-arrows metacategory, then \(\mathrm{Ar}(C)\), with its given partial composition, is an arrows-only metacategory whose units are exactly the identity arrows \(\{\mathrm{id}_b\}_{b \in \mathrm{Ob}(C)}\).
  2. If \((A, \circ)\) is an arrows-only metacategory, then \(\mathrm{Ob} := \{u \in A : u \text{ is a unit}\}\), \(\mathrm{Ar} := A\), \(\mathrm{dom}\) and \(\mathrm{cod}\) as in Definition 1, composition restricted to pairs with \(\mathrm{cod}(f) = \mathrm{dom}(g)\), and \(\mathrm{id}_u := u\), define an objects-and-arrows metacategory.
  3. These two constructions are mutually inverse.

Proof. (1) The Associativity and Identity Law axioms of \(C\), together with the fact that \(g \circ f\) is defined exactly when \(\mathrm{cod}(f) = \mathrm{dom}(g)\), give the Associativity axiom of Definition [Arrows-only] directly. For Existence of Units, given \(f : a \to b\) in \(C\), the arrows \(\mathrm{id}_b\) and \(\mathrm{id}_a\) satisfy \(\mathrm{id}_b \circ f = f\) and \(f \circ \mathrm{id}_a = f\) by the Identity Law, and each \(\mathrm{id}_c\) is a unit: \(\mathrm{id}_c \circ \mathrm{id}_c = \mathrm{id}_c\) by the Identity Law applied to \(f = \mathrm{id}_c\), and \(\mathrm{id}_c\) absorbs on both sides by the same law. These identity arrows are the only units. Suppose \(u\) is a unit of \((\mathrm{Ar}(C), \circ)\); write \(u : a \to a'\) in \(C\). Since \(u \circ u\) is defined, \(a = a'\), so \(u : a \to a\). Now \(\mathrm{id}_a \circ u\) is defined, so \(\mathrm{id}_a \circ u = u\) by the Identity Law; but since \(u\) is a unit and \(\mathrm{id}_a \circ u\) is defined, \(\mathrm{id}_a \circ u = \mathrm{id}_a\) as well. Hence \(u = \mathrm{id}_a\).

(2) Associativity for the constructed data is immediate from Definition [Arrows-only] together with Lemma 3. The Identity Law, for \(f\) with \(\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{cod}(f)} := \mathrm{cod}(f)\) and \(\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{dom}(f)} := \mathrm{dom}(f)\), reads \(\mathrm{cod}(f) \circ f = f\) and \(f \circ \mathrm{dom}(f) = f\), which is exactly Definition 1.

(3) Starting from \(C\), converting and converting back recovers the same objects (by the classification of units in (1)), the same \(\mathrm{dom}\) and \(\mathrm{cod}\) (since \(C\)'s own \(\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{dom}(f)}, \mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{cod}(f)}\) already satisfy Definition 1, hence coincide with the reconstructed ones by Lemma 1), and the same composition. Starting from \((A, \circ)\), converting and converting back recovers the same class \(A\), the same units (by (1)'s argument, a unit of the reconstructed metacategory is exactly \(\mathrm{id}_u = u\) for \(u\) an original unit), and the same \(\circ\), whose domain of definition is unchanged by Lemma 3. ■

¶ 2. Incidentally, this "arrows-only" reformulation exemplifies the structuralist ethos underlying category theory, in which objects are characterized entirely by their relational properties within the network of arrows, rather than by any intrinsic internal content. Within this established foundation, one takes a category to be any interpretation of the metacategory axioms within a standard (ZFC) set-theoretic background. To formally define a category, we follow the same steps used to define a metacategory in the beginning of this section:

Definition 2 (Direct Graph). A direct graph is a set \(O\) of objects, a set \(A\) of arrows and two functions \[ A \underset{\mathrm{cod}}{\overset{\mathrm{dom}}{\rightleftarrows}} O. \] The set of composable pairs of arrows is the set \[ A \times_O A = \{ \langle g,f \rangle \mid g,f \in A \; \text{and} \; \operatorname{dom}(g) = \operatorname{cod}(f) \}, \] denoted by product over O.

Thus:

Definition 3 (Category). A category is a graph with two additional functions, \[ \begin{aligned} O &\xrightarrow{\operatorname{id}} A, & A \times_O A &\xrightarrow{\circ} A, \\ c &\longmapsto \operatorname{id}_c, & \langle g, f \rangle &\longmapsto g \circ f, \end{aligned} \] called identity and composition, such that, for all \(a \in O\) and composable pairs of arrows \(\langle f,g \rangle \in A \times_O A\), the Associativity and Unit axioms and following properties holds: \(\operatorname{dom}(\operatorname{id}_a) = a = \operatorname{cod}(\operatorname{id}_a)\), \(\operatorname{dom}(g \circ f) = \operatorname{dom}(f)\) and \(\operatorname{cod}(g \circ f) = \operatorname{cod}(g)\).

Conventionally, the collection of objects of a category \( \mathscr{C} \) is denoted by \(\operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\), and the collection of its arrows (henceforth, morphisms) by \(\operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\). For any two objects \(a,b \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\), the collection of morphisms \(a \rightarrow b\), called a hom-set, is denoted by \(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{C}}(a,b) := \{ f \in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C}) \mid \operatorname{dom}(f) = a \text{ and } \operatorname{cod}(f) = b \}\).

Remark 1 (Small and large categories). A category \(\mathscr{C}\) is small if \(\operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\) and \(\operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\) are both sets, and large otherwise; it is locally small if \(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{C}}(a,b)\) is a set for every \(a,b\), irrespective of the size of \(\operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\) itself. Mac Lane (the main resource for this section) resolves the resulting set-theoretic difficulties, e.g. \(\mathbf{Set}\) is locally small although its objects do not form a set, by working relative to a fixed universe.
Remark 2 (The duality principle). To every category \(\mathscr{C}\) one associates its opposite \(\mathscr{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\), with \(\operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C}^{\mathrm{op}}) := \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\) and \(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{C}^{\mathrm{op}}}(a,b) := \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{C}}(b,a)\), composition being inherited from \(\mathscr{C}\) in reverse order. Since the axioms of Definition 3 are symmetric under this reversal, Mac Lane's duality principle asserts that every valid statement of category theory, expressed solely via objects, morphisms, composition, and identities, remains valid upon systematically reversing all arrows, i.e. upon replacing \(\mathscr{C}\) by \(\mathscr{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\) throughout.
Example 1 (Discrete Category). A category \(\mathscr{D}\) is said to be discrete if its only morphisms are the identity morphisms. Formally, for every \(f \in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{D})\), there exists an object \(a \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{D})\) such that \(f = \operatorname{id}_a\).
Example 2 (Monoid). A monoid can be equivalently defined as a category with a single object, say \(\star\). Consequently, any two morphisms in \(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathscr{C}}(\star, \star)\) can be composed. Thus, a monoid is characterized as a set \(M = \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\) equipped with a binary operation \(M \times M \rightarrow M\) (representing morphism composition) which is associative and admits an identity element. In other words, a monoid is a semigroup with an identity element.
Example 3 (Groupoid). A category \(\mathscr{G}\) is a groupoid if every morphism is invertible: for each \(f : a \to b\) there exists \(f^{-1} : b \to a\) with \(f^{-1} \circ f = \operatorname{id}_a\) and \(f \circ f^{-1} = \operatorname{id}_b\). A discrete category (Example 1) is trivially a groupoid, each identity being its own inverse; a one-object groupoid is precisely a group, exactly as a one-object category is a monoid (Example 2). A groupoid is thus the common refinement of Examples 1 and 2, obtained by additionally requiring invertibility.

¶ 2. Furthermore, a small category can be elegantly defined as a monoid object in the category of directed graphs (or spans) over a fixed vertex set \(O = \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\).

Proposition 1 (Categories as internal monoids). Let \(\mathscr{C}\) be a small category (Remark 1), and set \(O := \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\), \(M := \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\), equipped with \(s := \operatorname{dom}\), \(t := \operatorname{cod} : M \to O\). Form the fibered product (or the pullback) of \(M\) with itself over \(O\), \[ M \times_O M := \{ (g,f) \in M \times M \mid s(g) = t(f) \}, \] the set of composable pairs, and, iterating, the set \(M \times_O M \times_O M\) of composable triples. Definition 3 then states precisely that:
  • (Composition) there is a map \(\circ : M \times_O M \to M\);
  • (Associativity) the two induced maps \(M \times_O M \times_O M \to M\), given by \((h,g,f) \mapsto (h \circ g) \circ f\) and \((h,g,f) \mapsto h \circ (g \circ f)\), coincide;
  • (Identity) there is a map \(\operatorname{id} : O \to M\) such that \(\operatorname{id}_b \circ f = f = f \circ \operatorname{id}_a\) whenever \(s(f)=a\), \(t(f)=b\).
Equivalently: \(M\), together with \(s,t : M \to O\), is a monoid object in the category \(\mathbf{Graph}(O)\) of graphs with fixed vertex set \(O\) (equivalently, endo-spans \(O \xleftarrow{s} M \xrightarrow{t} O\) in \(\mathbf{Set}\)), with respect to the monoidal product \(\times_O\) and unit the identity span \(O \xleftarrow{\operatorname{id}} O \xrightarrow{\operatorname{id}} O\). When \(O\) is a singleton, \(\times_O\) reduces to the ordinary product \(\times\), and one recovers exactly Example 2; in other words, a small category is, precisely, the many-object generalization of a monoid.

¶ 3. Beyond these structural and somewhat fundamental examples, category theory furnishes the natural language for several fields in physics and mathematics, such as constructions central to both quantum mechanics formalism and quantization, both geometric and algebraic.

Example 4 (Category of Hilbert Spaces). In the categorical formulation of quantum mechanics, one studies the category \(\mathbf{Hilb}\), whose objects are complex Hilbert spaces and whose morphisms are bounded linear operators. It carries a symmetric monoidal structure given by the (completed) Hilbert-space tensor product \(\otimes\), essential for describing composite quantum systems and entanglement. Moreover, the assignment \(f \mapsto f^\dagger\) of the adjoint operator defines, in the language of Remark 2, an identity-on-objects functor \((-)^\dagger : \mathbf{Hilb}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathbf{Hilb}\) with \((-)^{\dagger\dagger} = \operatorname{id}\); this involutive structure, standard in categorical quantum mechanics, singles out the physically distinguished self-adjoint morphisms (observables) and unitary isomorphisms (symmetries).
Example 5 (Symplectic Category and Lagrangian Correspondences). Weinstein's symplectic category \(\mathbf{Symp}\) has as objects symplectic manifolds \((M,\omega)\). A morphism from \((M_1,\omega_1)\) to \((M_2,\omega_2)\) is a canonical relation: a Lagrangian submanifold \(L \subset \overline{M_1} \times M_2\), where \(\overline{M_1} := (M_1,-\omega_1)\). Given \(L_1 \subset \overline{M_1} \times M_2\) and \(L_2 \subset \overline{M_2} \times M_3\), their composite is defined, as for ordinary relations, by \[ L_2 \circ L_1 := \{ (x,z) \mid \exists\, y \in M_2,\ (x,y)\in L_1,\ (y,z)\in L_2 \}, \] which is Lagrangian provided the defining fibered product is transverse (or clean); absent this hypothesis \(L_2 \circ L_1\) may fail even to be a smooth submanifold, so that \(\mathbf{Symp}\) satisfies Definition 3 only generically — a defect repaired by Wehrheim and Woodward through the formal adjunction of composites. The symplectic category nonetheless furnishes the intended domain of a (still largely conjectural) quantization functor \(Q : \mathbf{Symp} \to \mathbf{Hilb}\), assigning to each classical phase space a Hilbert space of quantum states.
Example 6 (Category of C*-Algebras and Gelfand--Naimark Duality). Let \(\mathbf{C^*Alg}\) denote the category of unital C*-algebras and unital *-homomorphisms. Its full subcategory \(\mathbf{CommC^*Alg}\) of commutative unital C*-algebras is, by the Gelfand--Naimark theorem, dually equivalent to the category \(\mathbf{CompHaus}\) of compact Hausdorff spaces: the functor \(C(-) : \mathbf{CompHaus}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathbf{CommC^*Alg}\), sending \(X\) to its algebra of continuous complex-valued functions, is an equivalence. This is a concrete instance of the duality principle (Remark 2), in which \(\mathbf{CompHaus}^{\mathrm{op}}\) is equivalent to a category of an entirely different, algebraic nature, and it renders precise the passage, central to quantization, from a commutative algebra of classical observables to a possibly noncommutative one. Every Hilbert space \(H\) yields the C*-algebra \(B(H)\) of bounded operators; although \(H \mapsto B(H)\) is not functorial on all of \(\mathbf{Hilb}\), it restricts to a functor from the wide subgroupoid of \(\mathbf{Hilb}\) whose morphisms are unitary isomorphisms to the subgroupoid of \(\mathbf{C^*Alg}\) spanned by *-isomorphisms, via \(u \mapsto (T \mapsto uTu^{-1})\), thereby relating this example to Example 4.
Example 7 (Cobordism Category and TQFTs). Fundamental to the axiomatization of Topological Quantum Field Theories (TQFTs) is the cobordism category, denoted by \(\mathbf{Cob}_n\). The objects are closed, oriented \((n-1)\)-dimensional manifolds, and the morphisms are equivalence classes of \(n\)-dimensional oriented cobordisms between them. Composition of morphisms corresponds to the topological gluing of manifolds along their boundaries.

¶ 4. Until now, we have worked with the notion of maps between objects within a category, namely, the aforementioned morphisms (or, more abstractly, arrows, in the case of metacategories). There is, however, a broader notion: that of morphisms between categories themselves. Such morphisms are called functors. Formally, we define:

Definition 4 (Functor). Let \(\mathscr{C}\) and \(\mathscr{D}\) be categories. A functor \(\mathrm{F}: \mathscr{C} \rightarrow \mathscr{D}\) with \(\operatorname{dom}(\mathrm{F}) = \mathscr{C}\) and \(\operatorname{cod}(\mathrm{F}) = \mathscr{D}\) consists of a pair of functions \((\mathrm{F}_\mathrm{ob}, \mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{ar}})\), where \(\mathrm{F}_\mathrm{ob}\) acts on elements of \(\operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\) as \(T(a) = Ta \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{D})\), and \(\mathrm{F}_\mathrm{ar}\) acts on elements of \(\operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\) as \((a \xrightarrow{}[f] a') \mapsto (\mathrm{F}f:\mathrm{F}a \rightarrow \mathrm{F}c')\) in such a way that \[ \mathrm{F} \operatorname{id}_\mathscr{C} = \operatorname{id}_{T_\mathscr{C}} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathrm{F} (g \circ f) = \mathrm{F}g \circ \mathrm{F}f \] for any composable pair \(\langle f, g \rangle \in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\).
Remark 3 (Notation). The use of the notation \(\mathrm{F}_\mathrm{ob}\) and \(\mathrm{F}_\mathrm{ar}\) were due to pedagogical purposes. But, henceforth, and to avoid cumbersome notation, we will refer to both as \(\mathrm{F}\).

Analogously, a functor can be described in an "arrow only" framework, where \(\mathrm{F}\) is treated as a function that maps arrows \(f \in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\) to arrows \(\mathrm{F}f \in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{D})\), mapping identity onto identity and, for each composable pair \(f \circ g \in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\), one has a associated composable pair \(\mathrm{F}f \circ \mathrm{F}g \in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{D})\).

Example 8 (Quantization as a functor). The symplectic category \(\mathbf{Symp}\) has symplectic manifolds as objects and, in the construction described above, suitably composable Lagrangian correspondences as morphisms. A quantization procedure may be organized as a functor \(Q:\mathbf{Symp}\longrightarrow\mathbf{Hilb}\). At the level of objects, \(Q\) assigns to a classical phase space \((M,\omega)\) a Hilbert space \(Q(M,\omega)\) of quantum states. At the level of morphisms, a Lagrangian correspondence \(L:M_1\longrightarrow M_2\) is assigned an operator \(Q(L):Q(M_1,\omega_1)\longrightarrow Q(M_2,\omega_2)\). An essential requirement is that they respect the categorical structure: \[ Q(1_M)=1_{Q(M)}, \qquad Q(L_2\circ L_1)=Q(L_2)\circ Q(L_1) \] whenever the composite \(L_2\circ L_1\) is defined. Thus the functor turns composition of classical processes into composition of quantum operators. It is worth emphasizing that the notation \(Q:\mathbf{Symp}\to\mathbf{Hilb}\) denotes a particular quantization functor only after a quantization construction satisfying these properties has been specified. The categorical point is purely abstract, and it aims only to structurally organize and establish the construction of such functor.
Example 9 (Topological quantum field theory). Consider the cobordism category \(\mathbf{Cob}_n\), whose objects are closed oriented \((n-1)\)-dimensional manifolds and whose morphisms are \(n\)-dimensional oriented cobordisms, up to the chosen notion of equivalence. An \(n\)-dimensional topological quantum field theory is organized as a symmetric monoidal functor \(Z:\mathbf{Cob}_n\longrightarrow\mathbf{Hilb}\). At the object level, \(\Sigma\longmapsto Z(\Sigma)\) assigns a Hilbert space to each spatial manifold. At the morphism level, a cobordism \(M:\Sigma_0\longrightarrow\Sigma_1\) is assigned an operator \(Z(M):Z(\Sigma_0)\longrightarrow Z(\Sigma_1)\). Gluing cobordisms corresponds to composition of operators: \[ Z(M_2\circ M_1) = Z(M_2)\circ Z(M_1), \] and the identity cylinder on \(\Sigma\) is sent to the identity operator on \(Z(\Sigma)\). The monoidal structure adds further compatibility. Disjoint union of manifolds is sent to the tensor product of Hilbert spaces, \[ Z(\Sigma_1\sqcup\Sigma_2) \cong Z(\Sigma_1)\otimes Z(\Sigma_2), \] and disjoint union of cobordisms is sent to tensor product of the corresponding operators.

¶ 5. There are some special kinds of functors worth mentioning before we venture forth:

  • a functor \(\mathrm{F}: \mathscr{C} \rightarrow \mathscr{D}\) is forgetful when it forgets some algebraic structure(s) of objects of \(\mathscr{C}\). For example, the functor \(U: \mathrm{Grp} \rightarrow \operatorname{Set}\) assigns to each group \(G\) the set \(UG\) of its elements, forgetting the multiplication --- and, hence, the group structure --- defined on \(G\).
  • a functor \(\mathrm{F}: \mathscr{C} \rightarrow \mathscr{D}\) is full when, for each pair \(c, c' \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\) and for every arrow \(\mathrm{F}c \xrightarrow{g} \mathrm{F}c'\), there is a arrow \(c \xrightarrow{f} c' \in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\) with \(g = \operatorname{F}f\).
  • a functor \(\mathrm{F}: \mathscr{C} \rightarrow \mathscr{D}\) is faithful when, to every pair \(c, c' \in \operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\) and to every pair \(c \xrightarrow{f_1, f_2} c'\in \operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\), the equality \(\operatorname{F}c \xrightarrow{\mathrm{F}f_1 = \mathrm{F}f_2} \operatorname{F}c'\) implies \(f_1 = f_2\). An example of functor that is both full and faithful, is the functor \(2\text{-}\mathrm{Vects} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{F}} \mathrm{Ch}_{[-1,0]}(\mathrm{Vects})\). In particular, \(\mathrm{F}\) is also an almost surjective functor, and establishes the equivalence that \(\mathrm{F}\) is an equivalence of categories \(\Leftrightarrow\) \(V^\bullet \xrightarrow{f} W^\bullet\) is a quasi-isomorphism, a particular case of the Dold-Kan theorem.
Definition 5 (Natural Transformation). Consider two functors \(\mathrm{F}, \mathrm{G}: \mathscr{C} \rightarrow \mathscr{D}\). ¶ 6. Natural transformations may also be examined from another, equally interesting perspective. If one considers that functors create pictures of their source category within a target category, each picture being denoted by the functor itself, then \(\mathrm{F}\) is a picture of \(\operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\) and \(\operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\) in \(\mathscr{D}\), and \(\mathrm{G}\) is likewise a picture of \(\operatorname{Ob}(\mathscr{C})\) and \(\operatorname{Mor}(\mathscr{C})\) in \(\mathscr{D}\). A natural transformation may then be regarded as a collection of translations between such pictures, where the maps \(\eta_a, \eta_b, \eta_c, \dots\) as defined above act as the translators, rendering the corresponding collection of commutative diagrams.
