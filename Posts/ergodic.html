To Play Is Not to Watch: Ergodicity, Agency and the Deaestheticisation of the Video Game

Published: 2026-11-27

There is a confusion that resurfaces with irksome regularity in debates on video games, particularly on social media platforms such as \(\mathbb{X}\): the notion that watching a playthrough on YouTube is equivalent, in some relevant sense, to ‘consuming’ the game. The confusion is understandable—the image is there, the sound is there, the narrative unfolds, albeit incompletely, since the narrative component of a video game is not an autonomous stream but the product of a dialectical synthesis between ergodic performance and diegesis. Nor is it accidental: it flourishes within an apparatus of platforms that organises what is to be seen and what may be said about it, and in which having seen tends quietly to pass for having experienced. It rests, however, on a fundamental misapprehension of the nature of video games.

The misapprehension is best grasped by beginning with what any work of art is owed. A work possesses an internal organisation according to which its parts are articulated in a specific configuration, governed by a logic of its own. This formal law—what Theodor W. Adorno, in Aesthetic Theory, terms the work’s immanent law of form—is the principle by which each element of the work is what it is because the others so determine it, such that to alter it would entail the disfiguration of the whole. The success of a work is accordingly measured twofold: by whether it integrates its thematic strata and details into this immanent law and, in so doing, preserves that which resists integration, together with the fissures the process produces. To ask what it means to experience a work adequately is therefore to ask what its law of form demands of the one who encounters it. And the question that the confusion above never poses is precisely this: what is the law of form of the video game?

To answer it, one must turn to Espen Aarseth and his notion of ergodic cybertext, formulated in Cybertext: Perspectives on Ergodic Literature (1997). Aarseth appropriates the term ‘ergodic’ from physics, deriving it from the Greek ergon and hodos (‘work’ and ‘path’). A cybertext is a textual system whose structure integrates three inseparable dimensions: the immanent organisation of the text (its internal grammar, its generative syntax); the technical substrate in which it is physically instantiated (the book, the computer, the console or any other device, computational or analogue); and the active role of the reader as configurative operator of the system. The ergodic text is one whose traversal demands non-trivial effort—effort that exceeds hermeneutic interpretation and includes operations affecting the very manifestation of the text. Aarseth sharpens the distinction by negation: in non-ergodic literature the effort of traversal is trivial, imposing upon the reader no extranoematic responsibilities beyond, for instance, eye movement and the periodic or arbitrary turning of pages. Whereas the performance of the conventional reader takes place entirely in the mind, the user of a cybertext also performs in an extranoematic sense, effectuating a semiotic sequence through a work of physical construction for which the various concepts of ‘reading’ do not account.

Here, interpretative participation is distinguished from configurative participation. In a novel or short story, the reader certainly ‘interacts’ with the work—anticipating developments, establishing intertextual connections, re-signifying passages in the light of subsequent readings, forming judgements about the events presented, and so forth—but this interaction is hermeneutic: it unfolds on the plane of meaning, and the reader remains structurally powerless before the material sequence of the text, which is predetermined and immutable. Aarseth renders this powerlessness through two images: like a spectator at a football match, the reader may speculate, conjecture, extrapolate and even shout abuse, but is not a player; like a passenger on a train, the reader may study the shifting landscape and even pull the emergency brake, but is not free to move the tracks in another direction. Denied the player’s pleasure of influence, the reader enjoys only the pleasure of the voyeur—‘Safe, but impotent’ (Aarseth 1997: 4). In the ergodic cybertext, this changes. There, the reader-operator acquires configurative agency, and his or her actions effectively alter the manifestation of the text, determining which sequences will be revealed, by which paths, and in what order. Aarseth’s typology registers this shift in terms of user functions: beyond the interpretative function present in every text, certain texts additionally require an explorative function (the user must decide which path to take), a configurative function (scriptons are in part chosen or created by the user) or even a textonic function (textons or traversal functions may be permanently added to the text). The cybertext thus raises the stakes of interpretation to those of intervention, exposing its operator to a risk—that of failure or rejection—from which the reader of linear narrative is exempt. That risk is the first sign of what will later prove decisive: the cybertext does not yield itself; it resists.

To formalise this, Aarseth distinguishes between textons—strings of signs as they exist at the structural level of the system—and scriptons—strings of signs as they are actually presented to the operator in the act of traversal. Mediating between the two is what he calls the traversal function: the mechanism by which scriptons are revealed or generated from textons and presented to the user. In a CRPG such as Златогорье 2: Холодные небеса, Magic & Mayhem or Arcanum: Of Steamworks and Magick Obscura, every narrative and ludic node exists as a texton inscribed in the structure of the system; yet only those actually realised in a given playthrough—as a function of the choices made and the branches traversed by the reader-operator (here, the player)—manifest themselves as scriptons. In Aarseth’s terms, such works are dynamic texts: their set of textons is fixed, but the number and combination of scriptons vary according to the progress of play, as in his own example of Adventure (Crowther and Woods 1976). It would be too weak, however, to describe the system of textons as a mere catalogue of possibilities awaiting realisation, as though each scripton lay ready-made in storage and play merely retrieved it. The structure is better understood as a virtual field—fully real without being actual—which differentiates itself only in and through traversal, each actualisation producing something that did not pre-exist it as such. The experience of the game is this transformation, mediated by the ergodic effort of the operator. This configurational ontology is what grounds the specificity of the video game as a medium, and it is, in the strict sense, the content of its law of form. It also grounds Aarseth’s critique of the methodological colonisation of games by narratologies and film theory. As he observes, nothing is gained by such ‘theoretical imperialism’: when much energy is expended in demonstrating that a phenomenon is a perfectly deserving instance of some established category, the more fundamental question of what that phenomenon actually is tends to be neglected.

What, then, is actualised? Following C. Thi Nguyen’s argument in Games: Agency as Art (2020), the aesthetic content of the video game resides precisely in the agency temporarily inhabited by the player. For Nguyen, a game specifies a goal, designates the abilities with which that goal is to be pursued, and packages both with a set of obstacles crafted to fit them; goals, abilities and environment are the means by which designers practise their art, and we experience that art by flexing our own agency to fit. Games are, accordingly, a technique for inscribing forms of agency into artefacts—for recording, preserving and transmitting them—such that the corpus of existing games constitutes ‘a vast library of agency’ (Nguyen 2020: 19). The ludic diagram prepared by its creators is thus composed of sterile signs (ornamental and atmospheric elements, scenography, etc.) and signs of force (potentialities, mechanics, configurative possibilities that may be modelled both as graphs of states and transitions and as topological spaces of navigation and action, etc.), which are to be actualised by the operator in the process of ergodic human–interface–machine interaction. To play is therefore to submit to a practice in which the subject works upon its own conduct: it takes up ends that are not its own, sets aside others, and accepts a regimen of constraints in order to become, for a while, a different kind of agent. In this the player fulfils, almost literally, the comportment that Adorno, following Hegel, called freedom towards the object—the subject seeking not to make the work like itself, but to make itself like the work. Nguyen is explicit that the aesthetic qualities so produced are, in a decisive sense, first-personal: an observer may appreciate the elegance of a chess move that escapes a trap, but the harmony between one’s abilities, stretched to their limit, and the challenges of the world is an experience available primarily to the players themselves. What is at stake, he insists, is not an aesthetics of skilled performance viewed from without, but an aesthetics of agency—of deciding and acting as experienced by the agent. Kirkpatrick arrives at a convergent conclusion from the standpoint of critical theory: the sensations of gameplay are neither exclusively nor even primarily visual, but depend upon the structuring of time as a mode of imminent action. Such actualisation is, by definition, non-transferable: it exists only within the experience of the one who plays.

By contrast, a gameplay video constitutes a linear, non-ergodic metatext—that is, a documentary residue of another person’s interaction with the original cybertext. Walter Benjamin’s essay on the work of art in the age of its technical reproducibility supplies a useful distinction here: the photograph of a painting and the photograph of an event staged in a film studio are different kinds of reproduction, for in the former the thing reproduced is a work of art, whereas in the latter it is a performance, an event that is not itself the work. The playthrough belongs to the second kind. What it reproduces is not the system of textons and its traversal function, but a single, already concluded traversal—a fixed sequence of scriptons, detached, as every technical reproduction is, from the here and now of its occurrence and carried to meet the viewer in his or her own situation. It is an actual severed from the virtual that engendered it: where each act of play is a repetition that produces difference, the recording repeats without difference, returning the same sequence identically with every viewing. To watch a playthrough is therefore to consume the performance of another—a simulacrum that retains the imagistic and narrative surface of the game but subtracts precisely that which defines the video game as a medium: ergodicity. The configurative agency and the non-trivial effort that constitute the aesthetic experience of the work are, consequently, effaced.

To make this distinction more precisely, we can resort to Arthur C. Danto’s method of indiscernible counterparts in The Transfiguration of the Commonplace (1981), where objects that are perceptually indistinguishable may nonetheless possess radically distinct ontological affiliations, and the difference between them need not be visible. The images on the spectator’s screen may be pixel for pixel identical to those once seen by the player; the act of which they are the scriptons is not. Watching a gameplay video thus constitutes an activity ontologically distinct from the act of playing, inasmuch as it withdraws from the individual the very aesthetic object of the work. To expose the difference between the two does not, therefore, amount to passing a value judgement on the act of watching a playthrough. Aaron Smuts, in ‘Are Video Games Art?’ (2005), rightly notes that games are frequently played before audiences, much like dance contests or poetry slams, and that the player may plausibly be regarded as a performer within a larger performance; the spectator’s experience is therefore a legitimate aesthetic experience in its own right. It is simply the experience of a performance, not of the ergodic work. To recognise this is merely to acknowledge a fundamental difference between these experiences and, more importantly, the particularities of the videogame as a autonomous artistic medium.

The seed of such online discussion therefore lies on the act of denying this difference, that is when watching is declared equivalent to playing. And it is here that the argument returns to its point of departure, for it is precisely when the formal objectivity of a work ceases to be recognised that the process Adorno named deaestheticisation (Entkunstung—literally, in the translator’s gloss, ‘the destruction of art’s quality as art’) begins. Its unmistakable symptom, he observes, is the urge to handle everything, to permit no work to be what it is, to narrow its distance from the beholder. Deaestheticised art ceases to appear as a bearer of truth and comes to be treated as a mere object of immediate gratification, onto which the spectator projects his or her own interests and affects instead of allowing the work to make its claim. The artwork thereby becomes a tabula rasa for subjective projection, stretched between two poles: a thing among things and a psychological vehicle for the spectator, who hears in it only a standardised echo of himself. The contemporary platform perfects both poles at once. It renders the game a thing among things and it constitutes the viewer as a subject whose relation to works is measured in hours watched and opinions emitted, producing at the level of discourse the very equivalence between seeing and experiencing that it presupposes. What is lost in this process is precisely friction: the resistance the work opposes to the subject, which preserves the non-identity between subject and object.

In the video game, riction takes on a much more concrete, and less metaphorical, tone. Because the law of form of the work necessarily includes ergodicity, configurative agency is the condition of existence of the artistic object and the very vehicle through which its aesthetic content is actualised; the resistance of the work is the resistance of the system to its operator—the risk of failure, the demand for non-trivial effort, the temporary submission to a sculpted agency. Whoever watches a gameplay video and claims to have ‘played’ the game therefore participates in a process of deaestheticisation of the work: that resistance is cancelled, the non-identity between player and system collapses into the identity of a spectator with an image, and the work is reduced to a sequence of scriptons available for immediate consumption. To recognise the video game as art—or to analyse it seriously, irrespective of that label—requires acknowledging its ergodic condition and taking it as one’s methodological point of departure. As Kirkpatrick puts it, video games are aesthetic objects before they are anything else, and they can be comprehended only by examining what they feel like to those who play them.Finally, before concluding, it should be noted that nothing said or defended in this text implies that video games are superior to other media, nor that watching gameplay videos is an unworthy pursuit. Aarseth himself warns that debates over the standing of a given medium readily degenerate into apologetic claims and chauvinistic counterclaims; the present argument seeks to avoid both. It holds solely that the ergodic nature of the video game entails that experiencing it adequately—or ‘consuming’ it, in the idiom of \(\mathbb{X}\)—requires playing it. This does not constitute a supposed elitism, as many allege, but is simply a consequence of the ontology of the medium.
